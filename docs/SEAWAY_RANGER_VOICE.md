# Seaway interpretive voice — October 24, 2026

The visitor should leave knowing what to notice, why it happens, and how to keep exploring. Use warm, concrete invitations instead of adding jokes to control labels.

- Start with an observation: pick a mark on a lock wall; follow a ship's railing against it; trace the canal route around a river obstacle.
- Explain the mechanism: the vessel stays afloat while valves and gravity change the chamber's water level. The gate opens after the levels match. “Water elevator” describes the experience; its floor does not move.
- Explain the evidence: a received AIS position is a “radio postcard” with a place and a time. The analogy is not a promise of current visibility, full receiver coverage, queue order or arrival time.
- Invite a small experiment: step through a model, change beam/length, choose a staircase step or open a month of the historical logbook.
- Give each lock a distinct story. Preserve practical access instructions, documented viewing destinations, variable-lift caveats and the differences between reference values, illustrative models and live reports.
- Keep action labels short: Refresh, Clear ship filters, Google Maps directions, View entire Seaway. Retain the distinct loading, unavailable, filtered and quiet states.
