Before you start walking

Keep one picture in your head: saltwater west, freshwater east, one working complex between them.

Everything on this walk belongs to that picture. Boats have to change elevation. Freshwater has to be managed. Salmon have to continue inland. The most interesting visit is not checking off stops—it is noticing how all three problems are being solved in the same few acres.

At a lock chamberWatch the waterline on the wall. That is the easiest way to see the boat elevator working.
At the spillwayRemember that the complex is still working even when no boat is in a chamber.
At the fish ladderWatch a fish's rhythm—hold, push, rest—not just whether a fish appears in the window.

Three ideas worth carrying through the whole walk

Navigation

The boat is floating; the chamber is doing the work

The water beneath a vessel rises or falls until it matches the destination side. The wall, gates and waterline make that easier to understand than staring only at the boat.

Watch for: a fixed stain, ladder rung or wall feature that lets you judge how far the water moved.

Water control

The quiet infrastructure matters as much as the moving gates

The Locks are part of managing the freshwater Ship Canal and limiting saltwater intrusion. The spillway side makes that job visible.

Watch for: water moving through the system even when a chamber looks quiet.

Fish passage

The same barrier affects another kind of traffic

Returning salmon need a route around the elevation change. The fish ladder breaks that climb into smaller steps rather than one impossible obstacle.

Watch for: a fish holding in the current before it commits to the next move.

Research basis

Current operating, access and facility information is grounded in the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Salmon data comes from WDFW; live vessels come from AIS. Historic, public-art and neighborhood stops are used as visitor context. The present BallardLocks.org website is not used as an operational authority.
