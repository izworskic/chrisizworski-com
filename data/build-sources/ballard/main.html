Boats need to cross between them, salmon need a route inland, and the freshwater system has to be managed while the whole place keeps working.

The whole place in one idea

Don't think of the Locks as one attraction. Think of them as one working system doing three jobs at once.

Once you see those three jobs, the site starts to make sense. The lock chambers move boats between different water levels. The water-control structures help manage the freshwater system behind them. The fish passage gives migrating salmon a route around a barrier humans built across their path.

1 · Move boats

The chamber is the elevator

A boat is not hoisted by machinery. The water beneath it is raised or lowered until the chamber matches the water on the other side.

Watch for

Pick a mark on the lock wall and watch the waterline. The wall makes the change easier to see than the boat does.

2 · Manage freshwater

The Locks are working even when no boat moves

The Ship Canal and connected lakes are a managed freshwater system. Water control here is part of keeping that system within its operating range while limiting saltwater intrusion from the Sound.

Watch for

Look beyond the chambers toward the spillway and fish-ladder side. That is where the story stops being only about boats.

3 · Pass salmon

A migration has to cross the same barrier

Salmon returning from saltwater still need to continue inland. The fish ladder turns one large elevation obstacle into a sequence of smaller steps.

Watch for

In the viewing room, watch a fish hold in the current, push forward, then settle again. The movement is the story.

Understand the machine

Watch one complete lockage and the engineering becomes obvious

The useful trick is to watch the water, gates and wall—not just the boat. Tap each step before or during a lockage.

1

Boat enters

Traffic moves into a chamber that currently matches the water level it came from.

2

Gates close

The chamber becomes a controlled body of water separated from both sides.

3

Water moves

The chamber fills or drains until its surface matches the destination side.

4

Opposite gates open

With the levels matched, traffic leaves at the new elevation.

1 · Boat enters. Look at the waterline against the concrete before anything changes. That gives you a reference point for the whole cycle.
Read the landscape

Three things most first-time visitors can miss

These are the observations that turn a walk past infrastructure into an understanding of how the place works.

Compare the chambers

Same physics, very different scale

The small and large locks perform the same basic job for very different traffic. Stand where you can see both and compare the boat mix, wall height, gate scale and how much chamber surrounds each vessel.

Look up from your phone

If both chambers are active, don't choose immediately. Spend a minute comparing them before deciding where to stand.

Find the spillway

The quiet structure explains the lake

The chambers get the attention, but water control is part of the reason this complex exists. The live Ship Canal level will appear here when USACE data is available.

Look up from your phone

Face the fish-ladder/spillway side and notice that water management continues whether or not a lock gate is moving.

Watch the fish, not the count

The number tells you when; behavior tells you what

Current salmon context will appear here. During active runs, use the count to decide whether the viewing room deserves more of your time.

Look up from your phone

Watch for a fish holding position in the current before advancing. The pauses make the climb easier to understand.
