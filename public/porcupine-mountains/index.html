Lake Superior Circle Tour / Michigan's western U.P.

Find your way into the Porkies.

Above Lake Superior, the Porcupine Mountains rise in waves of hardwood forest. An old hemlock grove can hush the afternoon. The Presque Isle River thunders over dark bedrock. High above it all, Lake of the Clouds rests beneath a long, wooded ridge. This is a place to wander slowly. Your first glimpse of the wilderness

Three places worth making time for

Here is where the Porkies begin to reveal themselves: a quiet lake far below a rocky ridge, a river that fills the woods with its voice, and a lookout above a seemingly endless canopy. None requires seeing the whole park in one day. Pick the place that draws you in.

Eastern overlook · M-107

Lake of the Clouds

Step out toward the escarpment and the land seems to open beneath your feet. Lake of the Clouds lies far below, cupped by the wooded Carp River valley. In autumn, the hills glow with color; in summer, they rise in long green folds toward Lake Superior. Take time to look beyond the lake itself: this is an extraordinary sweep of forest that has endured for generations.

How to visit: The short overlook path offers the grand view without a long wilderness hike. The DNR describes approximately 100 yards of paved or boardwalk approach.

The longer Escarpment Trail is a separate, strenuous journey along the ridge. Check M-107 access and designated parking before setting out.

Western river corridor · County Road 519

Presque Isle waterfalls

You will often hear the Presque Isle before you see it. The river gathers speed between rock walls, spilling through Nawadaha, Manido and Manabezho falls on its way to Lake Superior. Walk slowly here. Each bend changes the view, and the mist, dark rock and rushing water make this a very different side of the mountains than the high overlooks.

How to visit: Treat the three falls as one river-country outing. Choose signed paths that suit your comfort with stairs, rocks and uneven footing.

The DNR documents an accessible platform for Nawadaha, not universal accessibility across the river corridor. Stay out of the river; its current and bedrock are dangerous.

Central forest · South Boundary Road

Summit Peak

The walk to Summit Peak begins under the shelter of northern hardwoods. Then the forest gradually falls away as you climb the tower, and the western Upper Peninsula opens around you. From up high, the view is not one single landmark but the pattern of the wilderness itself: wooded ridges, valleys and distant horizons.

How to visit: Allow time for the DNR’s half-mile forest approach, followed by the tower stairs. The trees along the way are part of the experience.

The approach and tower are not equivalent to a step-free scenic overlook. Seasonal vehicle access matters.

Go farther, look closer

The old forest is part of what makes this place rare.

Long before there were scenic roads or observation towers, these ridges were forest. Sugar maples and hemlocks still stand in old-growth groves where the light comes down softly and the woods feel remarkably quiet. The Porkies protect a remnant of the Great Lakes forest that visitors rarely have a chance to experience at this scale.

If you have a longer day, follow the history along the Union Mine interpretive trail, wander toward the Mirror Lake country, or plan a backcountry journey through the Carp River valleys. Each is a different landscape. Before walking beyond the short trails, check the official map, your return route, stream crossings and the time it will take on the ground.

Field notes · read the landscape

Four things the mountains will tell you, if you take your time.

There is a reason these woods feel different from a roadside scenic stop. Before choosing a trail, learn to read the ridge, the forest and the river. The more you notice, the more rewarding even a short visit becomes.

01The ancient ridge

Look past the lake to the shape of the land.

At Lake of the Clouds, the first instinct is to look straight down at the water. Then lift your eyes. The forested escarpment stretches across the horizon, and the lake lies sheltered on its landward side. Michigan DNR describes these mountains as remnants of a remarkably old mountain chain, with a ridge rising abruptly from the Lake Superior country. The view is more than a photograph: it is a chance to see how rock, height and time have shaped an entire watershed.

What to do: Start with the short, DNR-described overlook if that suits your group. The rugged Escarpment Trail is a different and more demanding decision, not an extension of the accessible platform.

Explore the lake overlookDNR: ancient escarpment and natural area
02The living forest

These trees are not simply a backdrop.

Walk a little way beneath the hemlocks and sugar maples, and the forest begins to feel larger than the trail. The Michigan DNR recognizes an approximately 35,000-acre block of primary, largely uncut forest here, among the most extensive remaining northern hardwood-hemlock forests in this part of North America. Look for the way hemlock keeps the understory shaded and fallen wood returns slowly to the soil. This is a living system that deserves your attention even when there is no waterfall or view at the end of the path.

What to do: Choose the Visitor Center Nature Trail for a slower introduction when trail conditions allow. The visitor-center building has seasonal hours; its closure does not by itself determine outdoor trail access.

Compare the forest outingDNR: old-growth wilderness
03The western river

Hear the Presque Isle before you see it.

The western park feels unlike the high lake overlook. In the designated Presque Isle scenic area, the river pours over low cascades, then narrows into swift channels cut through bedrock. Old-growth maple, hemlock and white pine shelter the gorge. Pause and listen to how the sound changes from a broad rush to a tighter roar. Nawadaha, Manido and Manabezho reward different viewpoints; no single photograph tells the whole river’s story.

What to do: Use signed paths and the official park map. DNR documents a separately accessible Nawadaha platform, not a barrier-free loop or a safe river crossing. The west-side road approach and final parking entry still require verification.

Meet the three waterfallsDNR: Presque Isle scenic area
04The wilderness decision

Know when the scenic road ends and the backcountry begins.

It is possible to enjoy the Porkies from an overlook and still leave with the feeling of having visited true wilderness. But beyond the short scenic approaches, the trails cross remote forest, hills and waterways. A map may name a path without proving its bridge, gate or stream crossing is usable today. In winter, roads that carry cars in summer can become snowmobile routes. Good wilderness judgment begins with knowing what the evidence cannot tell you.

What to do: Match your walk to your time and experience. Confirm official road notices, trail reports, parking and return logistics before departing, especially for the Escarpment or long backcountry trails.

Choose an experience, not an unverified crossing

Short walks with memorable rewards

The park's official descriptions provide useful viewing options that do not require inventing a cross-river hiking itinerary. Verify the specific road, trail and facility condition on your visit date.

Lake of the Clouds overlook

Approximately 100 yards of paved/boardwalk access from the scenic-area parking approach. Do not confuse this with the Escarpment Trail. M-107 is seasonally restricted.

Summit Peak tower approach

Officially described as a half-mile dirt-trail approach followed by stairs and the tower. It is not step-free; confirm seasonal access.

Nawadaha observation platform

DNR identifies an accessible viewing platform along the West River Trail. It does not certify the full East/West River circuit or a bridge crossing.

The DNR's regional managed-bridge database had just one vehicular/snowmobile bridge asset in the research area, with no condition value. It provides no current certification for these pedestrian crossings. Review bridge-asset data · Park access and facilities

What we still need before publishing hiking routes

The official GPS map identifies unbridged crossings, but those map symbols have not been independently matched to confirmed crossing coordinates. We are comparing exact DNR trail lines to Michigan's river centerlines; an intersection is only a candidate, not evidence of a bridge or ford.

The Escarpment, Government Peak, Lake Superior, and North Mirror Lake trails also have conflicting map and GIS mileages. Those disputes must be resolved before we supply end-to-end trail distances, elevation profiles, or hiking times.

How to read this guide with confidence

The geographic and access facts are drawn from the Michigan DNR. Neither the public GIS field “Open” nor a seasonal road calendar verifies conditions on the travel date. Trail distances, live road status, waterfall flow, and parking availability are deliberately not inferred. Check the park directly before your trip.

  • Michigan DNR — park services, access and current official links
  • DNR visitor guide (2025) — scenic-area approaches, trailer parking, Summit Peak
  • DNR park map — geographic districts, trail inventory and warning symbols
  • DNR public trail GIS — administrative data, not live trail confirmation
  • DNR parking-asset point inventory
  • DNR named recreation/trail reference point inventory
  • DNR designated wilderness and natural history
  • DNR current closure notices

The online tool does not replace the DNR park map, travel notices or backcountry preparation. A Recreation Passport is required for vehicle entry. The main planner is a preliminary itinerary aid, not navigation, emergency advice, official DNR endorsement or a certification of open access.
