Five ways to read the system

River reaches

Upper Manistee

Cold-water, intimate trout river with a dense access network and long float possibilities.

Middle / Hodenpyl

Broader river and backwater country tied to the Manistee River Trail and North Country Trail.

Tippy transition

Impoundment above the dam, then a major change in river character below it.

Lower Manistee

National Recreation River corridor, developed Forest Service access and migratory salmonid water.

Pine River

Cold, fast National Scenic River tributary with Forest Service carry-in landings and permit considerations.

The Little Manistee is available as an optional companion layer. It enters Manistee Lake and is intentionally not mislabeled as a direct Manistee River tributary.

Field guide

One river system, several different trips

The Manistee changes character repeatedly on its way west. A day on the upper trout water is not the same planning problem as a Hodenpyl backwater trip or a lower-river salmon run. This map keeps those reaches separate while still letting you see the system as one connected watershed.

For a trout day

Start with the Upper Manistee tab, then check the Grayling gauge for temperature and discharge. Use access provenance before building a shuttle, and open the current DNR regulation map for the exact reach you intend to fish.

For a paddle

Pick put-in and takeout on the same mapped river. The planner uses USGS NHD channel geometry for mileage and lets you set your own realistic travel speed. Pine River users should also confirm current Forest Service permit requirements.

For a camp / hike loop

Seaton Creek anchors the Hodenpyl end of the Manistee River Trail. On National Forest land, dispersed camping rules differ from designated riverside sites; the Forest Service says general dispersed camping is not allowed within 200 feet of water unless otherwise designated.

For the lower river

Tippy Dam is the hinge. Below it, the Forest Service-managed National Recreation River corridor includes High Bridge, Blacksmith Bayou, Bear Creek and Rainbow Bend access. Seasonal fishing pressure and boat traffic can be very different from the upper river.

Regulations without pretending the map is the law

Michigan's 2026 Fishing Regulations are in effect through March 31, 2027. The DNR's inland trout and salmon regulation map is the right place to identify Type 1–4, gear-restricted and other classified water. This tool links you there instead of copying a rule onto a marker and allowing it to go stale. The DNR's underlying 2026 stream-regulation GIS layer is based on Fisheries Orders 200 and 210 and was updated for the 2026–27 season.

Why the lines matter

The river geometry is not decorative. It comes from the USGS National Hydrography Dataset, which represents named flowlines and their network relationships. That lets the planner calculate a path along source-backed river geometry. If a point is too far from the mapped network or the network cannot connect the selected locations, the planner stops instead of substituting straight-line distance.

Tributaries: what is actually connected

The Pine River is a major Manistee tributary and an official National Scenic River. Bear Creek joins the lower Manistee and is recognized by the Forest Service as cold-water scenic-study water. The Little Manistee is useful context for anglers and paddlers in the same region, but it reaches Manistee Lake separately; the layer is therefore labeled “companion,” not “tributary.”

Data honesty

Three location-confidence labels are used. Agency coordinate means the managing agency publishes the location. Agency site · mapped coordinate means the facility is documented by an agency but the pin is taken from public geographic mapping. Community coordinate is reserved for useful access locations whose coordinates come from an identified local paddling source. That distinction is visible because a map should make uncertainty easier to see, not hide it.
