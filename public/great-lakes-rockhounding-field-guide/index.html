Shoreline Geologist Field Lesson · Coastal Dynamics

Gravel-Bar Anatomy: How Storm Waves Sort Great Lakes Cobble

Waves do not scatter rocks randomly. Hydraulic sorting operates by density, shape, and wave orbital velocity. When a gale blows onshore, breaking waves suspend fine sand and drag it lakeward into offshore sandbars, while pushing dense gravel shoreward into three distinct structural zones.

Zone 1 · The Active Swash

The Wet Sorting Edge

The narrow strip where breaking swash climbs and retreats. Capillary water covers the stones, eliminating diffuse surface reflection and causing mineral refractivity to pop. This is prime territory for spotting translucent red carnelian agates and the starburst corallites of Petoskey stones without needing a spray bottle.

Zone 2 · The Storm Berm

Peak Run-Up Windrows

The elevated ridge pushed 6 to 15 feet above normal water level during peak wave energy. As waves crash at maximum storm height, they fling heavy cobbles up onto the berm and drain through the gravel before they can pull them back. Look here for larger, heavier fist-sized agates, thick puddingstone chunks, and weathered husks.

Zone 3 · The Submerged Runnel

The Trough Lag Deposit

The shallow depression between the dry waterline and the first submerged bar (6 to 24 inches deep). Strong backwash drops dense silica minerals here while carrying lighter silt offshore. Wearing neoprene wading boots or polarized glasses to inspect this trough often uncovers stones completely missed by beach walkers.

Mineral & Fossil ID Diagnostics · What to Look For

Five Great Lakes Target Stones: Visual Field Diagnostics

Learn the geological age, diagnostic patterns, and dry-vs-wet cues before you step onto the beach.

Mohs 6.5–7 · 1.1 Billion Years

Lake Superior Agate

Portage Lake Volcanics · Chalcedony

Formed in basalt gas cavities during the Midcontinent Rift. Look for a pitted, orange-brown limonite rind ('peeler husk'), concentric fortification bands, and translucency when backlit against the sun.

Mohs 3 · 350 Million Years

Petoskey Stone

Devonian Traverse Group · Rugose Coral

Michigan's state stone: fossilized Hexagonaria percarinata colonial coral. When dry, looks like dusty gray limestone; when wet, stunning 6-sided polygonal corallites with radial starburst septa appear.

Mohs 3 · 350 Million Years

Charlevoix Stone

Devonian Tabulate Coral · Favosites

Often confused with Petoskey stones, but Charlevoix stones are fossilized honeycomb corals (Favosites). The corallites are miniature (1–2mm vs. 10mm in Petoskey) and tightly packed like delicate bee honeycomb.

Mohs 5.5–6 · 1.1 Billion Years

Yooperlite

Coldwell Alkaline Complex · Fluorescent

Glacial float from Ontario's Coldwell complex deposited on Superior beaches. Appears as ordinary gray granite-like syenite in daylight, but ignites in brilliant ember-orange under 365nm filtered UV light.

Mohs 7 · 2.3 Billion Years

Puddingstone

Lorrain Quartzite · Jasper Conglomerate

Ancient Paleoproterozoic glacial tillite. Vivid crimson, black, and maroon jasper pebbles naturally cemented into a bright white quartzite matrix. Extremely hard and takes an incredible natural polish.

Night Hunting Technical Specifications

UV-Light Specs for Yooperlites: 365nm Filtered vs. 395nm Cheap Lights

Not all ultraviolet flashlights work for rockhounding. Sodalite's fluorescent excitation curve peaks specifically around 365nm. Here is what you must understand before buying gear:

  • Why 365nm Longwave UV is Mandatory: 365nm photons directly excite the disulfide activator ions in sodalite, producing an intense 580–620nm yellow-orange emission. Cheap 395nm purple flashlights miss this excitation window and flood the beach in distracting violet visible light.
  • The Crucial ZWB2 Wood's Glass Filter: A raw 365nm LED still bleeds faint visible white/blue light. Premium rockhounding torches feature a dark black ZWB2 filter lens that absorbs all visible wavelengths, outputting pure blacklight. Fluorescent stones appear to glow out of pitch-black darkness.
  • Mandatory Eye Safety: Always wear clear UV400 polycarbonate safety glasses. While 365nm is UV-A, high-power emitters reflect invisible rays off wet cobbles directly into your retinas. Polycarbonate naturally blocks 99.9% of UV.
  • Field Protocol: Walk slowly with a white headlamp off; sweep the UV beam 3 to 6 feet ahead across the waterline gravel; watch for sparks of molten-gold fire.

Michigan Shoreline Jurisdictions

The Legal Boundary: Michigan's 25-Lb State Rule vs. National Lakeshore Bans

Great Lakes rockhounding is strictly governed by land ownership. Pocketing stones on the wrong stretch of coast can lead to federal misdemeanor citations. Know these exact boundaries:

  • Michigan State Land & Great Lakes Bottomlands (25 Lbs/Year): Under Michigan EGLE guidelines and Part 325 of NREPA, individuals may collect up to 25 pounds per calendar year of rocks, minerals, and invertebrate fossils for personal, non-commercial use on state-owned land, state parks, and public Great Lakes bottomlands lakeward of the Ordinary High Water Mark.
  • Federal National Lakeshores (STRICT BAN · 36 CFR § 2.1): Inside Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore and Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore, federal law strictly prohibits collecting or possessing even a single pebble or fossil. Fines range up to $5,000. Look, photograph, but leave them on the beach.
  • Township & Municipal Beaches: Governed by local municipal code. Where no local rock ordinance exists, the state 25-lb bottomland rule applies on the public water edge.
  • Private Shoreline Access: Under the landmark Michigan Supreme Court decision Glass v. Goeckel (2005), the public has the right to walk the Great Lakes shoreline lakeward of the Ordinary High Water Mark (OHWM), but you must access the beach via public property and respect private upland homes.

Eastern U.P. · Grand Marais · Lake Superior

Township Beach · 25 Lb Limit

Agate Beach, Grand Marais

Checking 72h wave work...
Lake Superior AgateJasperUnakite

Grand Marais sits in a premier natural littoral drift pocket where deep Lake Superior gales drive cobble eastward past Pictured Rocks and deposit it right against the bay's natural headland. This is the undisputed classic agate beach of Michigan. The gravel bars here run thick with amygdaloidal basalt clasts containing carnelian chalcedony nodules.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Face 350° due north into the surf with the sun behind you. Look for the 'peelers'—agate nodules where soft basalt matrix has weathered away to reveal waxy fortification banding. Hunt the wet waterline first, then inspect the fresh storm berm left at the base of the sand bluff.

If the Gale or Sleet Moves In: When 10-foot rollers make the beach unsafe, head two minutes into town to the Gitche Gumee Agate and History Museum on Coast Guard Point to study Karen Brzys' reference slabs under magnification.

Eastern U.P. · Luce County · Lake Superior

State Park · 25 Lb Limit

Muskallonge Lake State Park

Checking 72h wave work...
Lake Superior AgateYooperlite (UV)Jasper

Muskallonge is legendary among Michigan rockhounds for day-and-night versatility: premier agate picking during daylight hours, followed by world-class Yooperlite hunting after twilight. The park occupies a narrow strip of land separating inland Muskallonge Lake from the vast, open-fetch expanse of Lake Superior.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Hunt agates in the afternoon as western sunlight side-lights the cobble bars. After astronomical twilight, return with a 365nm filtered UV flashlight and clear safety glasses. Walk the damp gravel band 5 to 10 feet out from the swash to find syenite clasts glowing with fluorescent sodalite fire.

If the Gale or Sleet Moves In: Escape the raw open-lake wind by crossing County Road 407 to the sheltered south shore of Muskallonge Lake, or drive south toward Newberry along M-123 for hot pasties.

Shipwreck Coast · Luce County · Lake Superior

Public Beach · 25 Lb Limit

Crisp Point Lighthouse Beach

Checking 72h wave work...
Lake Superior AgateYooperlite (UV)Jaspilite

Crisp Point is wild, remote, and fully exposed to the longest unobstructed fetch on Lake Superior. With no offshore islands to blunt the swells, north-northwest gales fling 12-to-16-foot waves onto this prominent headland, relentlessly grinding and sorting thick glacial drift into coarse cobble windrows.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Inspect the steep storm berm pushed 15 to 20 feet up the beach profile. Peak storm waves push the densest silicate cobbles highest. Look for deep red jaspilite (banded hematite) and heavy fortification agates with distinct limonite cortex.

Good to Know Before You Drive: Reaching Crisp Point requires negotiating 18 miles of unpaved, seasonal county sand-and-gravel roads (CR-412/500). High clearance is recommended; the road becomes slick ruts during heavy rain or early November snow squalls.

Keweenaw Peninsula · Houghton County · Lake Superior

State Park · 25 Lb Limit

F.J. McLain State Park

Checking 72h wave work...
Lake Superior AgateEpidotePrehnite

Perched at the upper entrance of the Keweenaw Waterway, McLain's beaches sit directly over the billion-year-old Midcontinent Rift. Glacial ice plucked amygdaloidal basalt and copper-bearing minerals directly from the spine of the Keweenaw Peninsula, dropping them across these wave-battered cobble flats.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Walk the cobble shore north of the harbor breakwater after strong northwest blows. Scan for olive-green epidote cobbles, soft pink-and-green unakite, and basalt pebbles with round amygdules containing agate cores. Wet stones with lake spray to spot banding.

If the Gale or Sleet Moves In: Drive 15 minutes southeast into Houghton to visit the world-class A.E. Seaman Mineral Museum at Michigan Tech, home to the finest mineral collection in the Great Lakes basin.

Central U.P. · Marquette · Lake Superior

City Park · 25 Lb Limit Bottomlands

Presque Isle Park, Marquette

Checking 72h wave work...
Lake Superior AgateJaspiliteBanded Iron

Presque Isle is a spectacular tombolo headland where 1.7-billion-year-old dark peridotite meets Jacobsville Sandstone. While visitors flock to the black rock cliffs in summer, the pocket gravel coves along the neck and north curve collect extraordinary glacial float from the nearby Marquette Iron Range.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Look specifically for jaspilite: alternating ribbons of mirror-like metallic hematite and vivid red jasper from the Negaunee Iron Formation. It is dense, heavy in the palm, and takes an incredible polish. Stay well off the slick black cliffs during wave action.

If the Gale Moves In: Head into Marquette's downtown harbor district to warm up with fresh coffee, tour the Marquette Maritime Museum, and watch 1,000-foot ore carriers loading at the Presque Isle pocket dock.

Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore · Lake Superior

NPS BAN · LOOK ONLY · NO COLLECTING

Miners Beach, Pictured Rocks

Checking 72h wave work...
Agate (Observation)JasperChert

Miners Beach is an open-air geological showcase tucked beneath towering Munising Formation sandstone cliffs. But take heed: **COLLECTING IS STRICTLY FORBIDDEN**. Federal law (36 CFR § 2.1) bans removing any rocks or minerals from National Lakeshores, with steep fines for violators.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Because visitors are prohibited from taking stones, Miners Beach holds museum-caliber agates and colorful cherts right in plain view. Bring a macro lens or smartphone clip-on, a spray bottle of water to wet dry specimens, and photograph them in place.

Good to Know: Miners Beach stairs are well-maintained by the National Park Service. For rockhounding where you CAN legally collect, head 30 minutes east to Agate Beach in Grand Marais.

Little Traverse Bay · Emmet County · Lake Michigan

State Park · 25 Lb Limit

Petoskey State Park

Checking 72h wave work...
Petoskey StoneDevonian CoralFavosites

The definitive home of Michigan's state stone. Petoskey stones are fossilized colonies of Hexagonaria percarinata, a colonial rugose coral that thrived in shallow tropical seas 350 million years ago during the Devonian Period. Pleistocene glaciers plucked slabs from the Traverse Group limestone and rounded them into beach cobbles.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Hunt when the bay is calm after a southwest blow, ideally under an overcast sky or with the sun at your back. Wear waterproof footwear and step directly into 3 to 6 inches of water—the wet surface instantly illuminates the intricate starburst corallites.

If the Gale Moves In: Head into Petoskey's historic Gaslight District to explore the Little Traverse History Museum and browse local lapidary shops that specialize in hand-polished fossil corals.

Fisherman's Island State Park

Checking 72h wave work...
Charlevoix StonePetoskey StoneFavosites

Boasting six miles of undeveloped, low-profile Lake Michigan shoreline, Fisherman's Island is premier hunting territory for Charlevoix stones alongside Petoskeys. Leelanau Peninsula · Manitou Passage · Lake Michigan

Township Beach · 25 Lb Limit

Van's Beach, Leland

Checking 72h wave work...
Leland BluePetoskey StoneChert

Home of Michigan's most sought-after metallurgical artifact: Leland Blue. From 1870 to 1884, the Leland Lake Superior Iron Company smelted iron ore using local hardwood charcoal. The glassy slag byproduct—ranging from deep cobalt and turquoise to olive and purple—was dumped into the harbor, where Lake Michigan surf has tumbled it for over 140 years.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Leland Blue is an artificial silicate glass. Look for small spherical vesicles (trapped air bubbles), smooth conchoidal fractures, and rich sky-blue or aqua coloration that stands out against gray limestone gravel. Also yields genuine Petoskey stones.

If the Gale Moves In: Walk two blocks east into historic Fishtown along the Leland River to explore heritage fishing shanties, sample fresh smoked whitefish, or taste Leelanau wines.

Glen Haven Beach, Sleeping Bear Dunes

Checking 72h wave work...
Petoskey (Observation)Favosites

Nestled inside the protected hook of Sleeping Bear Bay, Glen Haven features an astonishing concentration of massive, pristine Petoskey stones. But remember the legal mandate: **DO NOT TAKE ANY STONES**. Federal regulation (36 CFR § 2.1) bans collecting on all National Lakeshore beaches.

Presque Isle County · Sunrise Side · Lake Huron

State Park · 25 Lb Limit

P.H. Hoeft State Park

Checking 72h wave work...
CrinoidsBrachiopodsStromatoporoids

The Lake Huron Sunrise Side is an invertebrate fossil hunter's paradise. Hoeft State Park features over a mile of sandy and cobble shoreline directly underlain by the fossiliferous Devonian Bell Shale and Rockport Quarry Limestone formations. Northeast gales roll unimpeded over deep water, dumping wave-worked fossils directly onto the beach.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Search the wet swash zone after northeast winds. Look for 'Indian beads' or 'fairy stones'—fossilized crinoid stem columnals—alongside ribbed brachiopod shells (*Atrypa* and *Mucrospirifer*) and wavy-banded stromatoporoid sponge heads.

If the Gale Moves In: Head 4 miles south to Rogers City to view the Calcite Quarry overlook—the world's largest open-pit limestone quarry—and watch freighters load high-calcium stone.

Alpena & Presque Isle · Lake Huron

State Rec Area · 25 Lb Limit (Hand Tools)

Rockport State Recreation Area

Checking 72h wave work...
Devonian Marine FossilsTrilobitesBrachiopods

Rockport is one of the most remarkable paleontology destinations in North America. While its Lake Huron coast features wave-worked limestone cobbles, the adjacent 300-acre decommissioned 1930s limestone quarry is an enormous open-air fossil field where millions of 380-million-year-old marine creatures weather out of soft shale benches.

The Veteran Geologist Move: Walk the gravel quarry flats with sturdy boots and gardening gloves. Look for intact brachiopods, delicate branching bryozoan fronds, rugose horn corals, and rare enrolled *Phacops rana* trilobites. Hand tools only; no power digging permitted.

If the Gale Moves In: Drive 20 minutes south to Alpena to tour the NOAA Great Lakes Maritime Heritage Center, an extraordinary free indoor museum featuring interactive shipwreck archaeology exhibits.

Shoreline Safety & Legal Ethics

How Veteran Beachcombers Hunt Responsibly

1. Respect the 25-Pound Annual Limit: Michigan's 25-pound personal limit exists so that future generations can experience the thrill of finding a Petoskey stone or Lake Superior agate. Take only what you will cherish or polish, and leave plenty for the next wave.

2. Absolute Zero-Tolerance in National Lakeshores: Rangers at Pictured Rocks and Sleeping Bear Dunes will issue federal tickets for taking stones. When visiting these national parks, take photos only and leave every rock where it lies.

3. Watch the Waves, Not Just the Rocks: Never turn your back on Lake Superior or Lake Michigan surf during post-storm conditions. Rogue swells and wave backwash can undercut steep gravel berms in seconds. Always hunt with a partner after dark.
