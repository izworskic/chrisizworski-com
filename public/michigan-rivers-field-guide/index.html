Reach #1 · Au Sable River Basin

Au Sable "Holy Water": Burton's Landing to Wakeley Bridge

Grayling, MI · 8.8 River Miles · 2.5–3.5 Hours Float
Running Clear · ~190 CFS Normal
River Gradient 3.2 ft/mi (Steady)
Current Velocity 2.5–3.0 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Gentle)
Permits Required None (DNR Passport)
USGS Gauge USGS 04135700

The 8.8-mile stretch from Burton's Landing downstream to Wakeley Bridge is the birth basin of Trout Unlimited (founded on these banks in 1959) and the gold standard of American spring-fed trout streams. Flowing over stable glacial outwash gravel at an almost unchanging 52° to 58°F, the Holy Water is governed by strict flies-only, catch-and-release artificial lure regulations.

For dry-fly anglers, this is hallowed ground: Hendrickson mayflies (Ephemerella subvaria) pop between 1:00 PM and 4:00 PM in May, followed by Sulphurs in June, and the legendary midnight Hexagenia limbata "Hex" hatch in late June and early July. For recreational paddlers and families, the current is smooth, forgiving, and crystal-clear with a firm gravel bottom and shallow sandbars ideal for children and sit-on-top kayaks.

Reach #2 · Au Sable River Basin

South Branch Au Sable: Chase Bridge to Smith Bridge

Roscommon, MI · 9.5 River Miles · 3.5–4.5 Hours Float
Running Clear · ~210 CFS Normal
River Gradient 3.8 ft/mi (Brisk)
Current Velocity 3.0–3.5 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Tight Cedar Bends)
Permits Required None (Wilderness Rules)
USGS Gauge USGS 04136500

Gifted to the state by automotive executive George Mason with the legal deed covenant that it remain an uncommercialized wilderness in perpetuity, the Mason Tract preserves 4,700 acres of pure pine barrens, towering white cedar swamps, and silent winding water. No permanent cabins, roads, or motors touch this reach.

Midway through the float sits the stone-and-timber Mason Chapel, an open river landing where paddlers pull out to sign the guest register in quiet reverence. The river here narrows through deep cut-banks and fast outside bends with sunken cedar logs ("sweepers") that require alert steering and basic ferry-gliding skills. Trophy brown trout hold tight beneath undercut cedar root-balls.

Reach #3 · Au Sable River Basin

Lower Au Sable National Scenic River: Foote Dam to Whirlpool

Oscoda, MI · 11.0 River Miles · 3.5–5.0 Hours Float
Steady Tailwater · ~1,350 CFS Normal
River Gradient 1.8 ft/mi (Broad)
Current Velocity 2.0–2.5 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Wide Water)
Permits Required USFS Day Pass ($5)
USGS Gauge USGS 04137500

Below Foote Dam, the Au Sable expands into a magnificent 150-foot-wide National Wild and Scenic River corridor flanked by towering 80-foot golden sand bluffs and old-growth white pines. This is big water, yet remarkably calm and free of dangerous log strainers, making it the premier family canoe, sit-on-top kayak, and SUP float on the Lake Huron side of Michigan.

In September and October, this tailwater transforms into Michigan's premier fall salmon and steelhead highway: massive Chinook salmon and chrome steelhead push upstream from Lake Huron to spawn on the gravel bars below Rea Road and Foote Site. Active bald eagle and osprey nests line the bluffs near Lumberman's Monument.

Reach #4 · Manistee River Basin

Upper Manistee Wilderness: Deward to M-72 Bridge

Kalkaska County, MI · 12.2 River Miles · 4.0–5.5 Hours Float
Running Cold · ~180 CFS Normal
River Gradient 4.0 ft/mi (Brisk)
Current Velocity 3.0–3.5 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Tight Cedar Turns)
Permits Required None (Wilderness)
USGS Gauge USGS 04123500

Originating in the sandy peat springs of Antrim County, the infant Manistee flows through the historic Deward ghost lumber town tract as one of Michigan's purest native brook trout streams. The water temperature rarely breaks 56°F even during July heatwaves, fed by hundreds of bubbling cedar seepages.

This is a narrow, intimate wilderness reach: the current is brisk, curling sharply around sandbars and cedar root balls. Paddlers must stay alert for low-hanging branches and submerged timber ("deadheads"). There is zero cell service from the put-in until near M-72. For dry-fly anglers, delicate presentations with 6X tippet and small parachute Adams or elk hair caddis produce eager, brightly colored wild brookies.

Reach #5 · Manistee River Basin

Middle Manistee: CCC Bridge to Sharon (M-66)

Fife Lake, MI · 14.0 River Miles · 4.5–6.0 Hours Float
Steady Flow · ~820 CFS Normal
River Gradient 2.4 ft/mi (Gentle)
Current Velocity 2.5 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Broad Gravel Flats)
Permits Required None (State Forest)
USGS Gauge USGS 04124000

The Middle Manistee from CCC Bridge down to Sharon is arguably the most relaxing, family-proven day float in northern Lower Michigan. Here, the river broadens to 90–120 feet with a continuous sand and fine-gravel bed, stable 2.5-mph current, and wide, gentle sweeping turns that leave ample time to navigate around any outer-bank woody structure.

CCC Bridge State Forest Campground at the put-in offers 24 rustic campsites directly overlooking the river, allowing paddlers to camp overnight and launch straight from their campsite in the morning. Shallow sandy islands midway through make ideal family picnic stops with waist-deep swimming water.

Reach #6 · Manistee River Basin

Lower Big Manistee: High Bridge to Bear Creek

Wellston, MI · 16.5 River Miles · 5.0–7.0 Hours (or Overnight)
Heavy Tailwater · ~1,850 CFS Normal
River Gradient 2.8 ft/mi (Heavy)
Current Velocity 3.5–4.0 mph
Rapids / Class Class I+ (Strong Eddy Lines)
Permits Required None (DNR Passport)
USGS Gauge USGS 04125550

Downstream of Consumers Energy's Tippy Hydro Dam, the Big Manistee becomes a massive, surging powerhouse—over 180 feet wide with heavy flow and deep turquoise pools reaching 15 feet down. This is the holy grail for Michigan drift boat guides chasing giant king salmon in September and October, and chromer winter steelhead straight through April.

For multi-day backcountry canoeists, expansive gravel islands and national forest bluffs provide some of the finest legal dispersed float camping in the Midwest. Build your campfire on the sand, watch the stars reflect off the black water, and wake up to morning mist rolling through the cedar bottoms. Due to heavy water volume, all paddlers must wear secure PFDs.

Reach #7 · Pine River Basin

Pine National Scenic River: Elm Flats to Low Bridge

Wellston, MI · 8.5 River Miles · 2.5–3.5 Hours Float
Fast Whitewater · ~275 CFS Normal
River Gradient 12.0 ft/mi (Steepest in LP)
Current Velocity 4.0–6.0 mph
Rapids / Class Class I–II (Standing Waves)
Permits Required USFS Watercraft Permit
USGS Gauge USGS 04125000

Tumbling down an astonishing 12-foot-per-mile gradient, the Pine River is the wildest, fastest, and most technical whitewater run in Michigan's Lower Peninsula. The water is ice-cold, carving through clay bedrock chutes, boulder fields, and tight s-turns with standing wave trains that will test your bracing, ruddering, and eddy-turn techniques.

Mandatory Federal Regulation: To protect this fragile National Wild and Scenic River canyon from erosion, the U.S. Forest Service enforces strict watercraft permits from the Friday before Memorial Day through Labor Day between 9:00 AM and 6:00 PM. Reserve your permit through Recreation.gov months in advance. Securely strap all dry bags and wear a high-flotation PFD.

Reach #8 · Pere Marquette Basin

Pere Marquette National Scenic: Green Cottage to Upper Branch

Baldwin, MI · 10.5 River Miles · 3.5–4.5 Hours Float
Running Clear · ~620 CFS Normal
River Gradient 3.6 ft/mi (Active)
Current Velocity 3.0 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Undammed Freestone)
Permits Required USFS Watercraft Permit
USGS Gauge USGS 04122500

The Pere Marquette is Michigan's crown jewel undammed freestone river—where German brown trout were first successfully stocked in the United States in 1884. Free of hydro dams from its headwaters all the way to Lake Michigan, the PM retains an untamed seasonal pulse: clear cold water, gravel spawning bars, and deeply undercut banks draped with ancient hemlock and white pine.

From M-37 down to Gleason's Landing, the river is designated Quality Trout Water with strict flies-only and artificial lure catch-and-release rules. Paddlers must obtain a mandatory USFS watercraft permit between Memorial Day weekend and Labor Day. The section between Green Cottage and Upper Branch Bridge provides a quintessential blue-ribbon float: lively riffles, dark holding pools, and zero development.

Downstream of Gleason's Landing, the Pere Marquette transitions from strict flies-only water into open gear regulations. Gleason's Landing USFS Campground offers riverside rustic sites, making this the ideal multi-day staging base. For paddlers and canoe trippers, the river features deep outside sweeping bends with wide sandy inside point-bars perfect for pulling up canoes and swimming in clean, refreshing current.

Reach #10 · Jordan River Basin

Jordan River Natural River: Graves Crossing to Rogers Road

East Jordan, MI · 9.0 River Miles · 3.0–4.0 Hours Float
Cold Spring-Fed · ~200 CFS Normal
River Gradient 4.5 ft/mi (Swift)
Current Velocity 3.0–3.5 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Tight Cedar Chutes)
Permits Required None (Natural River Rules)
USGS Gauge USGS 04127800

Designated in 1972 as Michigan's very first Natural River under the Natural River Act, the Jordan River flows through a glacial outwash valley of astonishing ecological purity. Fed by hundreds of subterranean cold-water artesian springs, the water temperature rarely exceeds 52°F, ensuring year-round nursery habitat for wild brook trout and sculpin.

Launching from Graves Crossing State Forest Campground, the river cuts through a cathedral of mossy northern white cedars. The current is brisk and clean with a firm sand-and-gravel bottom. Tight bends require basic steering control to avoid overhanging cedar boughs. For families, children, and fly anglers, it is one of northern Michigan's most visually breathtaking 4-hour river journeys.

Reach #11 · Pigeon River Basin

Pigeon River Wild Waters: Sturgeon Divide to Webb Road

Vanderbilt, MI · 7.2 River Miles · 2.5–3.5 Hours Float
Running Clear · ~85 CFS Normal
River Gradient 4.8 ft/mi (Freestone)
Current Velocity 3.0 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Boulders & Snags)
Permits Required None (P.R.C. Rules)
USGS Gauge USGS 04128900

Flowing straight through the 106,000-acre heart of the "Big Wild"—Pigeon River Country State Forest—this boulder-strewn freestone stream is Michigan's premier big-wilderness freestone trout water. The surrounding forest is home to the largest free-roaming wild elk herd east of the Mississippi (over 1,000 elk), plus black bears, bobcats, and bald eagles.

The Pigeon is wilder, rockier, and more unkempt than the gentle sand-bottomed Au Sable. Submerged granite boulders and natural log blowdowns create pocket-water feeding lanes where wild brookies and feisty brown trout aggressively strike dry flies and streamers. There is no cell service across this entire forest basin; bring a map, compass, and water filter.

Reach #12 · Upper Peninsula Basin

Two Hearted Wilderness: High Bridge to Lake Superior Mouth

Newberry, MI · 14.0 River Miles · 5.0–6.5 Hours Float
Wilderness Amber · ~130 CFS Normal
River Gradient 3.0 ft/mi (Wilderness)
Current Velocity 2.5–3.0 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Spruce Snags)
Permits Required None (State Forest)
USGS Gauge USGS 04044700

Immortalized in title by Ernest Hemingway (though he actually fished the Fox River in Seney for the story), the real Two Hearted River is one of the wildest, most solitary black-spruce water highways in the entire Upper Peninsula. Deep in Luce County, this amber-tinted stream flows over clean sandbars flanked by ancient tamarack bogs and blueberry plains.

The float culminates in one of the great geographic spectacles of the Northwoods: the river rounds a massive golden sand spit and empties directly into the roaring blue surf of Lake Superior. At the mouth, Two Hearted River State Forest Campground provides 39 rustic campsites right where the river meets the big lake. Target native coaster brook trout and migratory fall steelhead near the mouth.

Reach #13 · Upper Peninsula Basin

Hemingway's Fox River: Seney to Fox River SFC

Seney, MI · 10.5 River Miles · 3.5–5.0 Hours Float
Cold Peat Springs · ~160 CFS Normal
River Gradient 2.8 ft/mi (Meandering)
Current Velocity 2.5 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Cedar Sweepers)
Permits Required None (DNR Passport)
USGS Gauge USGS 04059000

In the late summer of 1919, a wounded 20-year-old Ernest Hemingway stepped off the train in the burned-over lumber hamlet of Seney, hiked north along the railroad tracks, and set up camp along the Fox River to heal from the traumas of World War I. That trip provided the emotional and sensory architecture for his masterpiece, "Big Two-Hearted River"—which he set on the Two-Hearted in name only because of its poetic cadence.

The real Fox River remains astonishingly wild and unchanged: tea-colored, spring-cold water curling past dense cedar hummocks, tag alders, and white birch. The river borders the 95,000-acre Seney National Wildlife Refuge, offering frequent sightings of sandhill cranes, trumpeter swans, and river otters. Wild brook trout rise eagerly to grasshoppers and caddis along the grassy undercut banks.

Reach #14 · Sleeping Bear Basin

Lower Platte River: Salmon Weir to Lake Michigan Mouth

Honor, MI · 4.5 River Miles · 1.5–2.5 Hours Float
Gentle Crystal Clear · ~125 CFS Normal
River Gradient 1.5 ft/mi (Shallow & Calm)
Current Velocity 1.5–2.0 mph
Rapids / Class Class I (Zero Rapids)
Permits Required NPS Park Pass ($25)
USGS Gauge USGS 04127200

Flowing straight through the southern boundary of Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore, the Lower Platte is without question the most relaxed, child-safe, and crystal-clear river float in the State of Michigan. Averaging only 2 to 3 feet in depth over a pure white sugar-sand bottom, this 4.5-mile float carries paddlers past Loon Lake, nesting loons, and dune bluffs without a single rapid, boulder, or dangerous log strainer.

This is the uncontested #1 Stand-Up Paddleboarding (SUP) river float in Michigan: the water is glass-calm, protected from Lake Michigan winds by dense pine dunes, and deep enough for long tracking fins while remaining shallow enough to step off onto soft sand at any point. The float ends on the pristine sandy beach at Platte River Point, where the warm river lazily mingles with the emerald swells of Lake Michigan.

Cedar Sweeper & Strainer Survival Guide: Cold-Water River Safety

Michigan trout rivers flow through dense cedar and hemlock swamps where fallen trees ("sweepers") and partially submerged branches ("strainers") pose the single greatest objective hazard to recreational paddlers. Water rushes through the branches like a sieve while pinning boats and paddlers against the wood with tons of hydraulic force.

  • Ferry-Glide Away from Outside Bends: River current accelerates around outside curves, drawing canoes directly into fallen cedar logs. Angle your bow 45 degrees toward the inside sandbar and back-paddle firmly to glide across the current away from the obstacle.
  • Lean INTO the Obstacle: If your canoe or kayak contacts a submerged log, your instinct is to lean away from it. This immediately exposes the upstream gunwale to the current, instantly swamping and capsizing the boat. Always lean downstream toward the log to allow the river to pass safely underneath your hull.
  • Wear a Fast-Drying PFD: Even on an 85°F July afternoon, northern Michigan spring-fed rivers flow at 50° to 56°F. Sudden immersion causes an involuntary cold-shock gasp reflex that can lead to immediate drowning without a properly fitted Life Jacket / Personal Flotation Device.
  • Cellular Dead Zones: Reaches like Deward on the Upper Manistee, the Pigeon River Country, and the Two Hearted River have zero cellular reception. Download offline Google Maps tiles and keep an emergency vehicle key pinned inside a waterproof dry box.

Frequently Asked Questions: Michigan River Paddling & Angling

Do I need a federal permit to paddle the Pine or Pere Marquette rivers?
Yes. The U.S. Forest Service requires mandatory watercraft permits on both the Pine River and the Pere Marquette National Scenic River from the Friday before Memorial Day through Labor Day. Daily craft caps apply between 9:00 AM and 6:00 PM to protect fragile riverbanks from overcrowding. Permits are reserved through Recreation.gov.
What are the gear rules on Michigan "Holy Water" flies-only sections?
On Michigan Quality Trout Waters (Type 4 or Gear-Restricted streams like the Au Sable Mainstream from Burton's to Wakeley, the South Branch Mason Tract, and Pere Marquette from Baldwin to Gleason's), anglers may only use artificial flies or single-pointed unweighted artificial lures. Harvest is restricted (catch-and-release only on designated sections), and scented synthetic baits are prohibited.
Can you camp for free along northern Michigan riverbanks?
Dispersed camping is permitted on state forest lands and Huron-Manistee National Forests, provided campsites are located at least 100 to 200 feet from the water's edge, away from boat landings, and outside designated no-camping zones (like the Mason Tract). State Forest Campgrounds along the rivers offer established fire rings and vault toilets for a nominal nightly fee.
What is the safest flow (CFS) for canoeing or kayaking Michigan rivers?
Stable groundwater feeds give rivers like the Au Sable (180–220 CFS at Grayling) and Manistee steady, predictable summer flows. However, fast-gradient streams like the Pine River become turbulent Class II whitewater during spring runoff above 350 CFS. Always check live USGS real-time gauges before launching.
How does two-vehicle self-shuttling work on Michigan rivers?
Drive both vehicles to the take-out landing first. Leave vehicle B parked in the designated day-use or trailer lot with your vehicle transfer gear. Drive together in vehicle A with your boats to the put-in launch. After completing your float, load boats onto vehicle B, drive back to the put-in to retrieve vehicle A. Always verify DNR Recreation Passport or USFS vehicle parking passes at both sites.
When does the famous Hexagenia limbata (Hex) mayfly hatch happen?
The massive Hexagenia limbata (giant yellow mayfly) emerges from silt beds during the last two weeks of June and the first week of July, typically between 9:30 PM and midnight on warm, muggy nights when water temperatures reach 64°–68°F. It brings the river's largest, wariest trophy brown trout to the surface.
