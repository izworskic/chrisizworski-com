Garden ecology

The extraordinary work of an ordinary-looking bee

A female transports pollen on brush-like hairs under her abdomen. She cuts flexible leaf pieces into tidy oval or semicircular shapes, carries them to a cavity and builds a succession of leaf-wrapped nurseries. Each receives pollen, nectar and one egg. She seals the chamber and begins another.

01

Gather leaves

Small, curved cuts in roses, lilacs and other leaves become lining for a nursery. This is normally cosmetic, not a plant-health problem.

02

Build a nursery

The female lines an existing tunnel with overlapping leaf pieces, adds food and lays one egg in each cell. She doesn't live with a worker colony.

03

Raise next summer's bees

The developing young feed inside sealed nests. Many species spend winter inside their cells, with exact developmental stage varying by species.

Pollination without inflated promises: leafcutter bees can be important visitors to summer flowers and some crops, notably alfalfa. They are not a substitute for every pollinator. Tomatoes rely substantially on self-pollination and buzz-pollination; bumblebees are better known for that job. No unsupported “100 times better” or “triple your harvest” claims here. Fear, gently corrected

There is no hive to defend.

Leafcutter bees are solitary. There are no worker bees guarding a shared store of honey or a queen. Females can sting, but they are not aggressive around their nests; incidents usually involve handling or trapping a bee.

Watch from a respectful distance. Don't squeeze bees, put your face in the nest or obstruct entrances. Anyone with a serious sting allergy should use appropriate personal precautions. Utah State Extension.

Garden mystery

Who's making crescents in my roses?

Not a caterpillar eating its lunch. The neatly clipped leaf edge often means a mother leafcutter is collecting construction material. She doesn't eat those sections.

Leave them alone when possible; the leaf is still doing its job. UC IPM considers the loss cosmetic on otherwise healthy plants. UC IPM.

Build a home that actually works

A little less bee hotel. A little more bee habitat.

The best first investment is flowers, pesticide protection and natural stems. For a nesting house, avoid a giant decorative hotel that cannot be cleaned. Small, replaceable tubes reduce the accumulation of disease and parasites.

Dry and secure

Mount firmly 4–5 feet above ground, facing roughly southeast for morning sun. Add a roof overhang and protect from driving rain.

Right-sized tunnels

Use clean, closed-back cavities 5–8 inches long with varied diameters. A 6 mm inner diameter is for commercially managed alfalfa leafcutters, not every wild species.

Easy to replace

Removable reeds, cardboard inserts, or opening trays allow annual rotation. Do not keep reusing permanently drilled, dirty tubes.

Flower-rich surroundings

Keep summer flowers close. MSU highlights bee balm, black-eyed Susan and purple coneflower. Avoid pesticide exposure and drift.

Research deep dive · Release & winter dormancy

You don't put adult bees to sleep. You protect the generation still developing.

This is the most important difference between summer leafcutters and spring mason bees. For commercially managed Megachile rotundata, larvae spin a cocoon and many enter diapause as mature prepupae. Later, after winter, development resumes and adult bees emerge. Other wild leafcutters can differ, and some produce a second summer generation.

Early summerPrepare

Mount clean nesting material and maintain open flowers. Wild native bees find the nest themselves; do not release purchased bees yet because of a calendar date alone.

Warm bloom windowRelease

For pre-incubated shipments, the retailer advises daytime weather consistently around 75°F with abundant blooms, and same-week placement.

After nesting endsLet brood mature

Wait until female traffic stops. Eggs and young larvae still need development time. A sealed tube does not prove the occupants are mature prepupae.

Fall to next springProtect dormancy

Keep occupied tubes dry, unheated, breathable and protected from predators. The adults from that generation generally emerge the following warm season.

Purchased and already incubated

Ready-to-emerge cocoons

These contain bees already approaching adult emergence. They cannot safely be treated like dormant larvae and put in the fridge until summer returns. Order for the week of abundant blooms and suitable weather; if conditions deteriorate before shipment, rearrange delivery with your supplier. If already delivered, follow the supplier's urgent handling procedure, not the fall-storage instructions.

Collected from last season

Dormant prepupae in leaf cells

These are developing young, not adult bees. For locally occurring wild bees in a home hotel, protect intact leaf-capped tubes in a dry unheated shelter and rotate old nests through an emergence box. For purchased alfalfa leafcutters in a cold Michigan winter, do not assume an unheated shed meets professional storage needs: prolonged freezing can be harmful to that managed species. For commercial M. rotundata production, specialist facilities sometimes store fully mature isolated cocoons under controlled refrigeration, roughly 39–50°F. Those protocols do not mean putting damp wild nesting tubes in a household refrigerator.

Canadian Food Inspection Agency · Utah State Extension

How do I know when it is time to put filled tubes away?

Look for a sustained end to female nesting flights, intact caps and the end of summer activity, rather than one cold night. Around 60°F daytime temperatures is a supplier's seasonal clue, not a biological switch. If still being filled, do not remove or rearrange active tunnels. Moving brood prematurely risks unfinished feeding and development. Later autumn removal protects from rain, predators and parasitic insects, but timing depends on actual activity. Keep capped ends facing up, use breathable pest-resistant storage, and monitor the first weeks for emergence of parasites.

Crown Bees fall protocol · USU Extension

Can I incubate last year's cocoons myself?

With confirmed mature managed M. rotundata prepupae and an appropriate storage history, warming restarts development. This takes weeks, not an afternoon. The commercial breeder reports roughly 20 days near 84°F and 42 days near 70°F; controlled academic work has reported approximately 27–32 days near 84°F for diapausing males and females. Actual emergence depends on sex, developmental condition and previous storage. Do not offer one exact countdown or warm unidentified native cocoons indoors. For first-time gardeners, pre-incubated shipments timed to flowering are simpler, if commercially raised bees are appropriate at all.

Crown Bees incubation explanation · Kemp & Bosch, 2001

Does an unheated Michigan shed work through winter?

For intact wild leafcutter nests, university extension guidance supports a dry, unheated garage, shed or similar protected setting with natural seasonal temperatures; an outdoor nest can also remain if sufficiently sheltered. Prevent wind-driven rain, condensation, rodents, birds and excessive solar heating. For purchased M. rotundata, professional cold storage is more tightly controlled and cannot be assumed satisfied by a backyard shed. SARE specifically advises avoiding extended freezing in managed alfalfa leafcutters: a poorly insulated Michigan shed can fall well below a managed colony's target range. Never seal occupied tubes in nonbreathable plastic or move them into a heated room. SARE commercial storage cautions.

USU Extension · CFIA biosecurity guidelines

Do not confuse the two kinds of cold storage: intact leafy home-nesting tubes are generally kept dry and naturally cool, not refrigerated in a typical kitchen. Commercially extracted, mature alfalfa leafcutter prepupae can be stored at controlled low temperatures by trained producers. Both statements can be true. Neither applies to bees already incubated and about to emerge. In a northern Michigan winter, prolonged freezing can be risky for commercially managed alfalfa leafcutters, even if native wild species overwinter locally.

Five steps to put occupied nesting tubes away safely

The house can stay outside; what matters is that the developing bees inside are not soaked, overheated, crushed, or brought indoors where they emerge months too early. These steps are for mixed wild nesting bees, not a replacement for a commercial species-specific cocoon management protocol.

  1. Wait until the females have finished. In late summer or fall, watch for the end of flights and sealed leaf-cell entrances. Do not collect actively used tubes. Michigan weather and species vary.
  2. Mark and keep occupied material intact. Write the year on the holder or label; handle gently without pulling apart cells, shaking, or freezing/defrosting them repeatedly. One tube can shelter multiple developing bees.
  3. Choose cool, dry, breathable protection. Keep tubes out of driving rain, condensation and rodent or bird access. A suitable unheated shelter or protected outdoor location can work; avoid a living room, warm garage, sealed plastic bag or damp cellar.
  4. Prepare clean nesting spaces for next year. Replace dirty or weathered empty tubes with clean, removable materials. Keep separate groups by year and avoid reusing old permanent tunnels.
  5. Let last year's bees leave before discarding tubes. Use a simple, ventilated emergence box with a small exit hole and fresh tubes available nearby. Follow MSU guidance, including proper orientation; local wild species may emerge at different times. Never cut open unknown occupied nests to harvest cocoons.

Commercial bee exception: dormant brood and ready-to-emerge shipped cocoons are not the same thing. Spring harvesting/storage directions for managed M. rotundata depend on supplier and development stage. Never refrigerate ready-to-emerge shipments as though they were dormant mason bees. For the most conservative first-year approach, invite wild bees instead.

The questions people actually ask

Leafcutter bees, without the myths

Will leafcutter bees sting me or my kids?

They are solitary and seldom sting except when trapped or handled; they do have stingers. Don't handle them, respect entrances and use appropriate precautions if someone has an allergy. There is no defensive honeybee-style colony.

Do I have to buy bees to get them?

No. Many wild species occur locally. Providing a small, well-managed cavity nest and flowers allows native local species to find the habitat naturally. Occupancy isn't guaranteed. Habitat and a pesticide-safe yard matter more than a shipment.

When should I put out the house in Michigan?

MSU recommends putting out bee hotels in late March through mid-April in Michigan, keeping them available through October. Leafcutter bees generally use cavities in summer, later than mason bees. Put out fresh, dry nest material before summer activity; this is not a release date for purchased cocoons.

At what temperature can I release purchased cocoons?

For commercially incubated M. rotundata cocoons, the seller recommends sustained daytime temperatures around 75°F, with abundant open flowers, and placement immediately after arrival. This is a supplier protocol, not a universal cutoff for wild bees. Don't use a USDA hardiness zone alone to select a date.

Can I put my leafcutter cocoons in the refrigerator?

Don't refrigerate ready-to-emerge purchased leafcutter cocoons: that is not the same state as dormant spring mason bees. Dormant brood in established nests has different seasonal storage requirements; commercial harvest/storage methods depend on developmental stage and source. For mixed wild bee nests, favor protected cool storage of intact tubes and tube rotation, following MSU's guidance.

What do I do with occupied tubes in fall?

After adults finish nesting and entrances are sealed, protect intact tubes from wet weather, predators and parasites. Keep them cool and breathable, not in a heated home; an unheated shed or sheltered outdoor location can work. Avoid disturbing or opening mixed wild species' cells. Replace/rotate used material after emergence in the next season.

Why don't bees use my beautiful hotel?

Possible reasons include no suitable local bees, too little floral forage, damp or moving tubes, unsuitable diameters, poor timing or pesticide exposure. Don't assume adding live cocoons fixes habitat problems. Xerces recommends natural nesting habitat as well as small managed houses.

Are these the bees that pollinate my tomatoes?

Not especially. Leafcutter bees do not perform the classic buzz-pollination important to tomatoes. Bumblebees excel at it, and tomatoes can often self-pollinate. Leafcutters have other roles in summer flower networks and certain crops.

How much will they improve my harvest?

No defensible fixed percentage applies to all home gardens. Outcomes depend on crop, other pollinators, weather, flower abundance and whether bee nesting occurs. Claims such as "100×" or "double harvests" should be treated as marketing unless tied to a specific controlled study and crop.
