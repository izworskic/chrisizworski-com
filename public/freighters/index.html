Know what you're watching

The working fleet

Recognize the ship before it reaches the harbor, even if you don't know the vessel's name.

Ⅰ

Lakers

Great Lakes-built bulk carriers haul ore, stone, coal, grain and other commodities. Many have the long discharge booms of self-unloaders.

Ⅱ

Salties

Ocean-going ships that transit the St. Lawrence Seaway to inland ports. A 1,000-foot laker is too long for the Seaway locks.

Ⅲ

Tug–barge combinations

Workboats and barges move fuel, stone and specialized loads through rivers and harbor approaches. They're part of the commercial fleet too.

Reading ship reports

Upbound generally means toward Lake Superior; downbound generally means toward the lower lakes. A ship's AIS destination may be abbreviated, stale or entered incorrectly. Public AIS reception has gaps, and a reported position is not permission to navigate by it.

Quick answers

Freighter-watching basics

Where can I see Great Lakes freighters right now?

Start with the live ship tracker. It has dedicated map views for the Soo Locks, Duluth, Port Huron, Detroit River, Saginaw Bay and Straits of Mackinac. Check a ship's most recent AIS timestamp; missing or stale reports do not prove no ships are present.

How do I know when the next freighter will pass?

Use a fresh AIS position together with a verified passage list or the specialist Soo Locks and Duluth tools. Position, course and estimated arrival can change. This hub does not generate arrival forecasts or assert that a particular ship will pass at a particular time.

What's the difference between a laker and a saltie?

A laker is built mainly for Great Lakes service and can be too large for the St. Lawrence Seaway. A saltie is an ocean-going vessel sized to use the seaway locks, connecting inland ports to world trade.

Can I use public ship-tracking data to navigate?

No. Public AIS feeds are for shoreside observation and can be delayed or incomplete. Use official navigational equipment and information at sea.
