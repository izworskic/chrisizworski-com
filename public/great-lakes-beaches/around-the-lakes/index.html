    A field guide to lake air

    Why the forecast can change at the water's edge

    Try a simple weather lesson when you visit: notice which way the wind blows as you leave trees or city streets for the sand. A breeze coming off cooler water may make the shore feel refreshing even when inland places are warm. Wind from land can tell a different story. Our National Weather Service reading describes the nearby area; it does not invent an exact temperature change at your feet.

    Each beach has its own character: open sand, high dunes, harbor structures or sheltered coves. Select the beach you hope to visit, check its official beach-health and access authority, and read the wind and water again when you arrive. For Michigan, the Beach Report provides 50 in-depth guides alongside this Great Lakes beach directory.

    Methods and limits

    Weather uses National Weather Service hourly forecasts from a separately identified nearby land grid. Temperatures are model forecasts, not measurements on the beach. Wind chill and heat index use the published NWS formulas when their inputs qualify. Each forecast must be recent; otherwise the number is withheld. No NOAA lake buoy is silently substituted for nearshore swimming water. NWS weather, beach-water testing, access rules and posted swimming flags are separate sources.

    Sources: NWS public forecast API, NWS lake-breeze science, NOAA offshore observations, and each destination's linked park and beach-health authority. EPA source-list year: 2025, retrieved November 24, 2026. Browse the official EPA BEACON map to identify a beach by its EPA ID. Some Michigan sites have no verified lake classification, and are available in All beaches search. The EPA listing is not a live open-beach or water-quality report.
