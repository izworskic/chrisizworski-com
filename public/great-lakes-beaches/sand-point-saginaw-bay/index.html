Listen for the lake breeze

Air coming across a Great Lake can change what the day feels like along the sand. Check the wind and water before leaving.

Wind: checking

The Sand Point peninsula reaches into shallow Saginaw Bay, giving wind direction special importance. One reach of shoreline may face more moving air than another. Look at the water where you actually plan to stand rather than borrowing a condition from a distant station.

Nearby NOAA lake temperature is being checked. It will not be mistaken for swimming-area water.

Where does this beach outlook come from?

The air temperature is a location forecast, not a thermometer on the sand. NOAA buoys report open-water conditions at their own coordinates. ShoreFeel does not invent a numerical beach-versus-inland cooling correction without field measurements.

Along the shore at Sand Point Beach

A shallow-water township beach on the Sand Point peninsula.

Read this shoreline in layers: a three-day weather forecast for the air, a recent NOAA observation for open-lake water at the station, and Michigan BeachGuard and NWS guidance for potential hazards. Each source tells a different part of the story. None replaces what you find at Sand Point Beach when you arrive.

Reading the weather together

Start with air comfort and the wind, then look at the waves, water observations and official notices. The Beach Day Score compares day-trip potential, not whether entering the water is appropriate. It is N/A unless the required weather values and both water temperature and wave height are present, with the NOAA observation no more than six hours old. Daily ranking also requires explicit low NWS swim risk and no matched official alert. Open the named sources, judge the station distance, and reassess the posted flag and shoreline conditions when you arrive.
