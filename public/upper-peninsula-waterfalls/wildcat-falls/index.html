Wildcat Falls

A small waterfall and a much bigger forest story. Walk a yellow-marked trail through old-growth cedar and hemlock to the rocky edge of Scott and Howe Creek, on land conserved for public enjoyment.

Owner-documented public walking trail; current opening and road conditions are unverified. The map uses the regional visitors bureau’s approximate waterfall location, never a parking GPS or a surveyed hiking path. Follow Northwoods Alliance’s signed arrival and illustrated trail map.

Before you drive

Land manager
Northwoods Alliance owns and manages Wildcat Falls Community Forest, which welcomes non-motorized public visitors. The roughly 200-acre conservation property includes a separate lake parcel; the waterfall has its own trail entrance.
Where to park
From Watersmeet, follow US-45 north → County Road 206 (Sucker Lake Road) west → County Line Lake Road north. Pass McGinty Lane and continue about 0.6 mile to a small hilltop. Look for the waterfall trail sign on the left. The owner describes roadside parking, not a GPS-surveyed visitor lot. Park only where legal and safe; conditions and available room are not live known.
Which trail?
Use the yellow-marked trail starting at the signed road entrance. Northwoods Alliance describes approximately ¾ mile of waterfall trail with uneven footing, mature cedar/hemlock, rock outcrops and creek overlooks. Its published number is not clearly labeled one-way or round trip, so do not interpret it as a measured round-trip distance.
How long?
Allow approximately 60–120 minutes as an editorial planning buffer for uneven terrain, photos and conditions; not an official trail timetable.
When does it look best?
After snowmelt or substantial rainfall. Scott and Howe Creek is a small watershed, so summer flow can be subtle. The mossy forest and old-growth character are compelling even without heavy water.

One map point, two different kinds of evidence

This map pin is the waterfall, not your arrival. The Western U.P. visitors bureau publishes a feature reference around 46.346173, −89.292236. Northwoods Alliance separately illustrates the falls and the public road trailhead, but does not provide a survey-grade trailhead coordinate. We intentionally do not draw an invented connecting hiking line or offer Google Maps car directions to the falls.

Why Wildcat is worth the walk

The attraction is not a giant drop. Scott and Howe Creek slips from wet upland forest into a sequence of small sandstone-like rock ledges and a narrow wooded ravine. Roots reach over stone, old cedars lean above the stream and hemlock canopy keeps the ground cool. The forest character, quiet, and the modest waterfall belong together.

Wildcat Falls was once part of the Ottawa National Forest. A land exchange threatened public access and fragmentation of the special site. After years of community conservation work, Northwoods Alliance secured the parcel as a Community Forest, protecting native habitats and a permanent public walking opportunity. The owner now describes a marked visitor trail instead of the bushwhacking directions still circulating in older online guides.

This is a hiking outing rather than an accessible boardwalk destination. Trail tread can be rooted and uneven, especially where it approaches damp rock. The owner does not establish a paved route, handrail, wheelchair access or a guaranteed close-up lower-falls vantage.

The right trail: why old directions fail

Follow the yellow markers from the signed trailhead. A formerly published description suggested walking west from the creek crossing without a maintained trail. That description predates the current community-forest trail and can send hikers away from the intended legal path. It should not determine your visit.

Northwoods Alliance's current illustrated map explicitly labels TRAIL HEAD on County Line Lake Road and FALLS near the western end of the marked woods loop. The property map shows the falls visit west of the road and separate from the parcel near County Line Lake. Stay on the designated public path and respect neighboring ownership. Do not attempt an off-trail river shortcut or scramble down a wet cliff to reproduce a photograph.

Read owner rules, signage and directions. The owner allows ordinary outdoor recreation, including properly licensed hunting and fishing in season, but prohibits fires, driving motor vehicles off public roads and collecting stone, plants or wood. Do not drink from the property's artesian spring without treating the water; the owner specifically warns against untreated consumption.

What changes by season?

Spring: Snowmelt makes a small creek more energetic, but trails can be muddy, slick and affected by blowdowns. No live flow or bridge inspection feed exists.

Summer: The old cedar-hemlock forest may be the strongest draw. Expect reduced cascade volume after dry weather, insects and humid rocky tread.

Autumn: Cooler hiking conditions and hardwood color contrast with dark evergreens. Leaves and rain can hide slippery stones; trail conditions are not monitored here.

November through winter: This page does not verify that County Line Lake Road is plowed, the signed roadside parking remains usable or the yellow trail is safe under ice. The land manager publishes public hiking permission, not a promise of winter vehicle access or a maintained frozen-waterfall trail. Make a different choice if the road or tread cannot be safely used.
