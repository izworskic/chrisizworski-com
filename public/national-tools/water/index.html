Flow and stage are different measurements

Discharge describes the volume of water moving past the gauge, while stage is water-surface height at that gauge. One should not be substituted for the other.

Trend often matters more than the snapshot

A river at the same flow can mean different things if it is rising quickly, falling after a crest or holding steady. The live tool shows recent change rather than only the latest number.

Historical context answers “unusual for now?”

Same-date historical comparisons can show whether current flow is relatively high or low for the time of year. They do not make a recreational safety determination.

Official flood forecasts stay official

When a gauge can be matched to NOAA’s National Water Prediction Service, the tool shows that forecast separately. Weather context alone is not treated as proof that a river will rise or fall.

Coastal water is a different decision

Beach risk, surf, buoy observations and tides do not belong inside an inland river gauge interpretation. The coastal tool keeps those sources separate and hands Michigan beach users to the deeper Great Lakes beach network.

Snowmelt is upstream context, not a river forecast

Measured snowpack and warm-weather melt pressure can explain one watershed input, but they do not prove when or how much a river will rise. Use the Snowpack tool for the pack itself and River Conditions for measured flow and official NOAA river forecasts.

Safety boundary: Gauge readings are environmental observations, not a paddling, swimming, wading or boating safety determination. Local hazards, access rules, water temperature and skills still matter.
