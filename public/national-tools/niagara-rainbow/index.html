What makes this different

A weather forecast cannot tell you where a rainbow will be

Niagara already supplies the water droplets. The missing pieces are direct sunlight and geometry. A primary rainbow appears about 42 degrees from the antisolar point — the point directly opposite the sun. This tool calculates the sun position every 10 minutes, estimates where the falls’ mist is being pushed by the wind, then evaluates that mist from five public viewpoints.

Direct sunlight is non-negotiable. Cloud cover is still decisive: perfect geometry does not help if a cloud blocks the sun, which is why the result is an opportunity score rather than a promise.

Model details and limitations

The model uses NWS forecast-grid cloud cover, wind speed, wind direction, precipitation probability and visibility when available. It approximates each falls’ mist source and plume displacement. Local spray is turbulent and clouds can change faster than a forecast grid, so real-world conditions can beat or miss the model.

Quick answers

Niagara Falls rainbow FAQ

What is the best time to see a rainbow at Niagara Falls?

There is no fixed hour. The best time depends on the season, clouds, wind and where you stand. The live forecast above identifies the strongest window instead of using a generic “morning” or “afternoon” rule.

Does it need to be raining?

No. Niagara Falls continuously creates a large mist plume. Sunlight hitting those airborne droplets at the correct angle can produce a rainbow on a dry day.

Which side of Niagara Falls is best?

Both sides can work. The model scores U.S. viewpoints such as Terrapin Point, Prospect Point and Luna Island alongside Canadian viewpoints such as Table Rock and Queen Victoria Park.

Why did the rainbow disappear even though the score was high?

A small cloud passing in front of the sun can shut the effect off almost instantly. Wind also moves the mist. Use the score as a decision aid, then use the live cameras above to see whether direct sun is actually hitting the spray.
