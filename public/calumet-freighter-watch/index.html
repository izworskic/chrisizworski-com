HOW THE INDUSTRIAL RIVER WORKS

Why does the roadway rise into the sky?

A freighter cannot squeeze beneath an ordinary road deck. At 95th Street, two bascule leaves pivot upward, counterweights helping the machinery raise the span. Farther inland, some railroad structures use vertical lifts, raising a whole span between towers.

This is a teaching illustration, not a drawing to scale, a real-time bridge state, or a signal that any particular bridge is opening.

FIELD NOTES / SOUTH CHICAGO

A port that is still at work.

The Calumet is not Chicago's downtown riverfront. A ship coming from Lake Michigan enters a waterway of lift spans, bascule leaves and working terminals. The Illinois International Port District has facilities at Iroquois Landing near the river mouth and farther inland at Lake Calumet. Those terminals serve commerce, not sightseeing access.

NOAA charts three turning basins along the channel, about 0.9, 4.63 and 6.06 miles upstream from the river mouth. Those wider water areas help ships maneuver; the mile references are documented, but this map does not invent precise turning-basin pins.

At the 95th Street Bridge, the machinery is the spectacle. Listen for the interruption in traffic, look at the counterweighted leaves and notice how slowly a long hull threads through the crossing. You may see no ships during your visit. The pleasure of a working port is knowing what the bridges and docks are built to accomplish.
