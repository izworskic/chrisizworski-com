How to read a snow report

  • The number is snowfall as reported at that moment, in inches. It is not automatically a 24-hour total.
  • The time matters as much as the number. A mid-storm report from 9 p.m. and a storm total from 7 a.m. at the same place can differ by half a foot.
  • Measured or estimated. Measured means the observer put a ruler in the snow; estimated means they did not.
  • The source tells you who reported it: trained spotter, CoCoRaHS volunteer, co-op observer, NWS employee or the public.
  • The place. NWS shorthand such as 4 NE Negaunee means four miles northeast of Negaunee; this page writes it out.
  • The remark is the observer's own note, often with the period covered, such as storm total or since snow began. Every row links to the original NWS text.

How much snow did my town in Michigan get today?

Type the town into the search box on this page. Every figure is a National Weather Service Local Storm Report: a snowfall measurement or estimate sent in by a trained spotter, a CoCoRaHS or co-op observer, NWS staff or the public, and published by one of the five NWS offices that cover Michigan. If the town sent its own report, you get its largest one in the chosen window, with the time, the source and the observer's remark. If it did not, you get the three nearest reports with how far away they are and in which direction, which is usually the honest answer after a storm, because reports come from observers rather than from every town. The number is what that observer had at that moment, so a mid-storm report can be passed later by a storm total from the same place. When nothing has been reported anywhere in Michigan, the page says so.

How much snow did Michigan get yesterday?

Use the Since yesterday view. It covers every report sent since midnight yesterday, Eastern time, which matters because some of the most reliable reports arrive the morning after a storm: CoCoRaHS volunteers take one daily measurement, ideally around 7 a.m., covering the previous 24 hours, so yesterday's snow often appears in this morning's reports. The view catches both the in-storm spotter reports from yesterday and those next-morning measurements, and each place still appears once, at its largest report. Before quoting a big number, read its time and remark: a remark such as storm total or since snow began tells you what period the figure covers, and a storm total sent this morning can include snow that fell the day before yesterday.

How much snow has Michigan had this season?

It depends heavily on where you are, which is why this page answers station by station instead of with one statewide number. The season table adds up daily snowfall since July 1 at 52 National Weather Service cooperative and airport climate stations and sets each total against that station's 1991-2020 normal for the same dates, so 40 inches in Marquette and 40 inches in Detroit read very differently. For scale, last winter (2025-26) ended with 366 inches at Herman in Baraga County, 176 percent of normal; 276.6 inches at the Marquette NWS office; 75.4 inches at the Grand Rapids airport; and 39.5 inches at Detroit Metro Airport. A station missing more than five snow-season days is marked incomplete rather than compared with normal, because a missing day can only hide snow. For a single city's official season total, the NWS climate report for that office is the record of reference.

How much snow does the Upper Peninsula get?

More than anywhere else in Michigan, but it varies across the U.P. more than most people expect, because Lake Superior lake-effect bands do the heavy lifting. The 1991-2020 normal season is 207.8 inches at Herman in Baraga County and 196.8 inches at the Marquette NWS office near Negaunee, while Marquette city on the shore averages 102 inches and Escanaba on Lake Michigan 46.9 inches. Last winter (2025-26) ran above normal at every complete U.P. station in the season table: Herman finished with 366 inches, 176 percent of normal, the Marquette NWS office with 276.6 inches, Chatham near Munising with 213.5, Ironwood with 193.4 and Sault Ste. Marie with 189.2. The highlands inland from Lake Superior collect the most and the southern U.P. toward Wisconsin and Lake Michigan the least. Once snow starts falling, the region filter on this page narrows live reports to the Keweenaw, the Central and Western U.P. or the Eastern U.P.

When does Michigan usually get its first snow?

Weeks earlier in the Upper Peninsula snow belt than downstate. Counting the first day with at least a tenth of an inch of snow at National Weather Service climate stations over the 1991-2020 seasons, the median first snow falls around October 14 at the Marquette NWS office near Negaunee, October 16 at Herman and October 22 at Sault Ste. Marie. Downstate it waits until about November 9 at the Grand Rapids airport and Houghton Lake, and November 20 at Detroit Metro Airport and the Saginaw-Bay City-Midland airport. Port Huron has the latest median of the stations measured, December 1. In an early year the first snow comes one to five weeks sooner than the median, and the earliest in those thirty seasons was September 22, at Herman and at Chatham near Munising. A first snow in October rarely lasts on still-warm ground, so it says little about the winter ahead. This page shows each season's first measurable snow as soon as a climate station records it.

Is there enough snow to snowmobile or ski in Michigan?

Snowfall totals cannot answer that on their own, because what fell is not the same as what is on the ground. A fresh six inches on warm or bare ground can settle, melt or blow away within a day, while a modest storm on top of an existing base can open a whole trail system. Riding and skiing depend on the depth and firmness of the snowpack, frozen ground and swamps, and grooming, which is why the reliable seasons are in the lake-effect belts of the Upper Peninsula and the northern Lower Peninsula. Use this page to see where new snow is falling and how the season compares with normal, then check measured snow depth and the trail reports before you load the sled or the skis. The snowmobile and cross-country ski tools linked from this page do that region by region.

Where does lake effect snow hit hardest in Michigan?

Lake effect snow forms when cold air crosses the relatively warm open water of the Great Lakes, picks up heat and moisture, and drops it in narrow, intense bands downwind. In Michigan that puts the heaviest lake effect along and inland from the Lake Superior shore of the Upper Peninsula, and across western Lower Michigan inland from Lake Michigan, reaching down to the southwest corner. Because the bands are narrow, towns a few miles apart can report very different totals from the same event, which is why this page ranks individual reports instead of averaging a county. Open-water temperature is the fuel, so the season usually fades as the lakes cool and ice over.

The Great Lakes Buoy Dashboard shows current water temperatures, the fuel side of that equation. Last winter's season table shows the pattern plainly: Herman, Big Bay and the Marquette NWS office in the Lake Superior belt all finished above 200 inches, while Port Huron, the Saginaw-Bay City-Midland airport and Detroit Metro Airport finished under 40.

Why do Michigan snow totals from TV, apps and this page disagree?

They are usually measuring different things at different times. A TV or app total may be a forecast, a radar estimate or a regional summary, while this page lists only ground reports sent to the National Weather Service, each tied to one place and one moment. A report taken mid-storm will be lower than the storm total from the same backyard hours later, an estimated report is less exact than a measured one, and lake effect bands can put very different amounts on neighboring towns. When you need one number for a decision, use the largest recent report closest to where you are going, check its time, and read the remark for the period it covers.
