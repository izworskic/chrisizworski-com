Michigan Rockhounding Conditions

The best time to hunt a Great Lakes beach is right after a storm, once the waves have settled. This page reads the wind, waves and marine warnings at 15 Michigan agate, Petoskey stone and fossil spots and tells you which ones were just turned over, which are still too rough, and which have gone quiet.

By Chris Izworski, Bay City, Michigan. Live conditions load when the page opens.

Best beach right now

Reading the lakes…

  • Freshly worked: go
  • Recently stirred
  • Quiet: picked over
  • Waves building: wait
  • Storm on: stay off
  • Not wave-dependent

Beach by beach

Best first. The strip under each beach is the wind blowing onto it over the last three days and the next two: tall bars are waves being driven onto the gravel, and the line marks now.

No beach on this list matches that combination. Try another stone, or include look-only beaches.

                                How the reading works

                                Michigan's geologists at EGLE give one rule for timing a hunt: go immediately after a storm, because new material is washed up, and do not go during the storm or when there is high wave action. Everything here is that rule, measured.

                                1. Which way the beach faces. For each beach we computed the direction of open water in front of it and how far that water runs, from the mapped lake shoreline. A north wind that slams Grand Marais barely touches Presque Isle in Marquette, which faces northeast.
                                2. How hard waves were driven onto it. Every hour of the last three days, we take the part of the wind blowing from the lake straight onto the beach, scale it by the open water it crossed, and count only the hours strong enough to move gravel. Wind is used because it is measured everywhere, all year; wave buoys are pulled out of the lakes every fall.
                                3. Whether it has settled. If the wind is blowing onto the beach right now it reads Waves building: wait. If that onshore wind is strong, or the National Weather Service has a gale, storm, beach hazards or lakeshore flood alert for the beach or its nearshore water, the beach reads Storm on: stay off. When a real blow has passed, it reads Freshly worked: go. The nearest NOAA wave buoy is shown when one is reporting.

                                This tells you where the lake has recently done the digging. It cannot tell you what any one beach is holding today, and the National Weather Service is the authority on safety: if waves are breaking hard, stay well back from the water and off breakwalls and wet rock.

                                What you are looking for

                                Lake Superior agate

                                Banded quartz that formed in gas pockets of lava flows from the Midcontinent Rift about 1.1 billion years ago, then was spread south by glaciers. Look for a waxy shine, iron reds and oranges, and stone that glows when you hold it up to the sun. Fine concentric bands are the giveaway; the outside is often pitted.

                                Jasper

                                The opaque cousin of agate: dense, smooth, brick red to brown and no light passes through it. Common on the same Lake Superior beaches, and often the first red stone that catches your eye.

                                Yooperlite

                                Plain gray syenite by day, glowing orange-yellow under a longwave 365 nm UV light at night because of the fluorescent mineral sodalite. Named in 2017 by Erik Rintamaki; most finds come from the eastern U.P. shore toward Whitefish Point. Wear UV-blocking glasses.

                                Petoskey stone

                                Michigan's state stone: Hexagonaria percarinata, a colonial coral from Devonian seas about 350 million years ago. Each six-sided cell has a dark center, and the pattern only shows when the stone is wet or polished, so hunt the swash zone or carry a spray bottle.

                                Charlevoix stone

                                Another Devonian coral, Favosites, often called honeycomb coral. The cells are smaller than a Petoskey's and lack the dark eye in the middle. Found on the same northern Lake Michigan beaches.

                                Leland Blue

                                Not a natural stone: blue and blue-green slag glass left by the iron smelter that ran at Leland in the 1870s and 1880s. Bubbles and a glassy shine give it away. Look along the Leland shoreline.

                                Devonian fossils

                                The limestone of northeast Lower Michigan is packed with sea life: ribbed brachiopod shells, crinoid stems that look like stacks of tiny washers, horn corals and lacy bryozoans. EGLE suggests looking right after a big wave event, when fresh fossils erode out.

                                What you can take home

                                • State land, including state parks and the public Great Lakes shore: EGLE limits rock, mineral and fossil collecting to 25 pounds per person per year in total, for personal use. Follow any posted park rule.
                                • City, township and county beaches: check for local collecting rules at the beach.
                                • National lakeshores (Pictured Rocks, Sleeping Bear Dunes) and Isle Royale: removing rocks, minerals or fossils is prohibited under federal law. Look and take photos.
                                • Private land: get the owner's permission first.

                                Questions

                                When is the best time to look for agates in Michigan?

                                Right after a storm on Lake Superior, once the wind has dropped and the waves have settled. Storm waves churn the beach gravel and bring fresh stones to the surface, and EGLE's guidance is to go immediately after a storm but never during one. Spring, just after the ice goes out, and fall, when storms are frequent and beaches are quiet, are the strongest seasons.

                                Where can I find Lake Superior agates in Michigan?

                                On Lake Superior gravel beaches across the Upper Peninsula. Agate Beach in Grand Marais, the shore from Muskallonge Lake State Park east toward Crisp Point, F.J. McLain State Park in the Keweenaw and Black River Harbor in the western U.P. are all public options on this page. Pictured Rocks beaches have agates too, but collecting there is prohibited.

                                Where is the best place to find Petoskey stones?

                                Northern Lake Michigan from Little Traverse Bay to Charlevoix and down the Leelanau and Benzie shore. Petoskey State Park, Fisherman's Island State Park, Wilderness State Park and the Frankfort public beach are public places to hunt. Sleeping Bear Dunes has them too, but it is illegal to remove stones from the National Lakeshore.

                                How many rocks can I take from a Michigan beach?

                                On state-owned land, including state parks and the public Great Lakes shoreline, EGLE limits collecting to 25 pounds of rocks, minerals and fossils per person per year in total, for personal use. Local parks can have their own rules, national lakeshores prohibit collecting entirely, and private land needs the owner's permission.

                                Is it legal to take rocks from Pictured Rocks or Sleeping Bear Dunes?

                                No. Both are National Park Service lakeshores, and federal rules prohibit removing rocks, minerals or fossils. Enjoy them where they lie and take photos instead.

                                How does this tool decide a beach was freshly worked?

                                It adds up the wind that blew straight onto each beach over the last three days, scaled by how much open water lies in front of it, and checks that the wind has since eased and no gale, storm, beach hazards or lakeshore flood alert is in effect. Strong onshore wind recently, calm now, reads as freshly worked. It measures the lake's work on the beach, not what any one beach is holding.
