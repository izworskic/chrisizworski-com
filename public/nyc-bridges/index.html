01 / 06The iconic walk

Brooklyn Bridge

Lower Manhattan ↔ DUMBO / Brooklyn Heights · 1883 · Hybrid suspension / cable-stayed

Step onto the elevated wooden promenade and the city slowly opens around you. Granite Gothic arches frame the harbor, while diagonal stay cables weave an intricate geometric web across the skyline.

Walk it
Since the permanent bike lane was shifted down to the vehicular roadway, the boardwalk is exclusively for pedestrians. Enter in Manhattan at City Hall Park (opposite Brooklyn Bridge-City Hall 4/5/6 and Chambers St J/Z) or in Brooklyn at Cadman Plaza East / Tillary Street. Mid-span stairways connect directly down to Prospect and Washington Streets in DUMBO.
Photograph it
Empire Fulton Ferry lawn in Brooklyn Bridge Park provides the quintessential lower harbor perspective, capturing the massive limestone piers against the Lower Manhattan financial district. On the bridge deck itself, early morning light striking the Manhattan tower offers pure diagonal drama.
Why it matters
Opened on May 24, 1883, John and Washington Roebling's masterwork was the first steel-wire suspension bridge in history, joining two independent cities and redefining civil engineering on an unprecedented scale.

02 / 06The photographer's frame

Manhattan Bridge

Chinatown ↔ DUMBO · 1909 · Suspension

Steel towers, roaring subway trains and wide-open East River vistas make this the energetic counterpart to the Brooklyn Bridge. It provides the finest panoramic view of the Brooklyn Bridge itself.

Walk it
Pedestrians use the dedicated South Walkway (access at Bowery & Canal St in Chinatown or Jay & Sands St in Brooklyn), while cyclists ride the separate North Path. Keep an eye out for wire-mesh cutouts specifically designed for cameras along the pedestrian path.
Photograph it
From the South Walkway looking southwest, the Brooklyn Bridge is framed perfectly in front of Lower Manhattan and One World Trade Center. At ground level, the world-famous intersection of Washington and Water Streets in DUMBO frames the Manhattan Bridge anchorage between historic brick warehouses.
Why it matters
Engineered by Leon Moisseiff using modern deflection theory and completed in 1909, it was designed from inception to carry heavy rapid-transit subway trains (B, D, N, Q) along its outer decks, serving as a primary lifeline for millions of commuters.

03 / 06Neighborhood character

Williamsburg Bridge

Lower East Side ↔ Williamsburg · 1903 · Steel-tower suspension

This is a working bridge with a raw, unmistakable neighborhood cadence: pink-painted steel trusses, rumbling J/M/Z trains, local commuters, vibrant street art, and sweeping vistas of the Midtown skyline.

Walk it
Enter the dedicated pedestrian walkway at Delancey and Clinton Streets in Manhattan (near Essex St F/M/J/Z) or at Roebling and South 4th/5th Streets in Brooklyn. Cyclists use the separate north bikeway starting at Clinton/Norfolk. Check NYC DOT notices for periodic maintenance lane shifts.
Photograph it
The Brooklyn waterfront at Domino Park offers an expansive ground-level view of the monumental lattice steel towers. On the bridge itself, the geometric patterns of the diagonal lattice trusses cast bold graphic shadows during the golden hour.
Why it matters
When completed in December 1903, its 1,600-foot central span was the longest suspension span in the world, breaking the Brooklyn Bridge's record and pioneering the use of all-steel construction towers instead of masonry.

04 / 06Engineering close-up

Ed Koch Queensboro Bridge

Midtown East ↔ Long Island City · 1909 · Double-cantilever truss

Unlike its graceful East River suspension neighbors, the Queensboro crosses on massive, interlocking cantilever trusses. Beneath its muscular steel geometry, Roosevelt Island, the tramway and Midtown emerge in vivid relief.

Walk it
Following extensive DOT safety reconfigurations, the South Outer Roadway serves as a dedicated, fully separated pedestrian promenade, while bicycles use the North Outer Roadway. Enter at East 60th Street & 2nd Avenue in Manhattan, or Queens Plaza North & Crescent Street in Long Island City.
Photograph it
The Long Island City waterfront along Gantry Plaza State Park and Hunter's Point South provides an unforgettable profile of the bridge's intricate silhouette against the Midtown skyscrapers. Roosevelt Island underneath provides an imposing look straight up into the cantilever cross-beams.
Why it matters
Designed by engineer Gustav Lindenthal and architect Henry Hornbostel and completed in 1909, it ranks among North America's greatest double-cantilever bridges, stepping across two river channels with Roosevelt Island as its central anchor.

05 / 06Hudson panorama

George Washington Bridge

Washington Heights ↔ Fort Lee, NJ · 1931 · Double-deck suspension

Here the city yields to the sheer drama of the Hudson River gorge: 212 feet above open water, the towering New Jersey Palisades on one bank and Manhattan's rocky cliffs on the other.

Walk it
Pedestrians cross on the Port Authority's reconstructed South Walk (reopened in late October 2026), featuring accessible approach ramps, widened pathways, and dedicated viewing platforms. Cyclists use the North Walk. Manhattan access is at West 178th Street & Cabrini Boulevard (near 175th St A train); New Jersey access is at Hudson Terrace in Fort Lee.
Photograph it
Fort Lee Historic Park perched atop the Palisades offers the defining panoramic overlook of the Hudson River, the steel framework of the towers, and the distant skyline of Lower Manhattan. On the bridge, the new observation bump-outs allow unimpeded river photos.
Why it matters
Engineered by Othmar Ammann and opened in 1931, its 3,500-foot main span nearly doubled the previous world suspension record. The exposed crisscrossing steel of its towers was originally meant to be encased in granite, but the raw steel was celebrated as a Modernist icon.

06 / 06The oldest chapter

The High Bridge

Washington Heights ↔ Highbridge, Bronx · 1848 · Roman-Style Aqueduct

Before New York built its iconic suspension bridges, it conquered freshwater scarcity by raising a Roman-style aqueduct 140 feet above the Harlem River. Today, The High Bridge stands as NYC's oldest surviving bridge—a car-free brick promenade connecting Manhattan and the Bronx.

Walk it
Operated by NYC Parks and open daily (7 a.m.–10 p.m. seasonally; check park gates in winter). Completely car-free and paved in brick. Manhattan access is inside Highbridge Park at West 172nd Street & Amsterdam Avenue (paved accessible ramp winding past the 1872 High Bridge Water Tower; take the 1, A, or C trains to 168th St). Bronx access is at University Avenue & West 170th Street (accessible ramp directly onto the bridge abutment; take the 4, B, or D trains to 167th/170th St).
Photograph it
The terrace beside the 179-foot High Bridge Water Tower on the Manhattan bluff affords a soaring vantage point looking eastward along the length of the span into the Bronx. Mid-span, compose the historic masonry arches against the 1927 steel arch, with Metro-North Hudson line trains gliding along the riverbank far below.
Why it matters
Completed in 1848 under chief engineer John B. Jervis as the crowning achievement of the Old Croton Aqueduct, it piped clean drinking water 41 miles from Westchester to save Manhattan from catastrophic cholera epidemics. In 1927, five stone river piers were replaced by a single 442-foot steel arch to widen Harlem River navigation. Following an extensive $61 million restoration, it reopened to the public in 2015.
