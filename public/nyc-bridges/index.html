01 / 06The iconic walk

Brooklyn Bridge

Lower Manhattan ↔ Brooklyn · 1883 · Suspension

Step onto the elevated promenade and the city slowly changes around you. Granite towers frame the river, while the cables form a remarkable web against the skyline.

Walk it
Begin on the signed pedestrian approach in Lower Manhattan or Brooklyn. The promenade is above the traffic; bicycles use a separate protected path.
Photograph it
Brooklyn Bridge Park’s Empire Fulton Ferry lawn provides a memorable view toward the towers and Lower Manhattan. The bridge walkway itself offers a second, very different composition.
Why it matters
Completed in 1883 from a design initiated by John A. Roebling, this crossing changed how the two cities met across the East River.

02 / 06The photographer's frame

Manhattan Bridge

Chinatown ↔ DUMBO · 1909 · Suspension

Steel towers, subway trains and a close view of the East River make this the energetic counterpart to the Brooklyn Bridge. It is an extraordinary place to watch a city in motion.

Walk it
A dedicated pedestrian walkway crosses between Manhattan and Brooklyn, separate from the bikeway. Check current DOT construction updates before setting off.
Photograph it
Washington Street in DUMBO frames the Manhattan Bridge between brick warehouses. Its famous composition is from the street, not from the bridge walkway.
Why it matters
Finished in 1909, the bridge carries trains as well as road traffic. The grand Manhattan entrance was designed by Carrère and Hastings.

03 / 06Neighborhood character

Williamsburg Bridge

Lower East Side ↔ Williamsburg · 1903 · Suspension

This is a working bridge with a neighborhood rhythm: steel trusses, trains, cyclists, and views toward the Lower East Side and Williamsburg.

Walk it
The bridge has a pedestrian walkway and a separate bikeway. NYC DOT has reported narrowed sections during rehabilitation; check notices before visiting.
Photograph it
For a broader portrait of the span, explore the public waterfront near Domino Park on the Brooklyn side, rather than promising an unobstructed viewpoint at every location.
Why it matters
When it opened in 1903, its main span set a world record. It still carries subway trains between Manhattan and Brooklyn.

04 / 06Engineering close-up

Ed Koch Queensboro Bridge

Midtown ↔ Long Island City · 1909 · Cantilever truss

Unlike its East River suspension-bridge neighbors, the Queensboro crosses on interlocking cantilever trusses. Beneath its heavy geometry, Roosevelt Island and the river come into view.

Walk it
The south outer roadway became a pedestrian-only walkway in 2025, while the north outer roadway serves cyclists. Construction may affect approaches; verify current NYC DOT instructions.
Photograph it
The Long Island City waterfront offers a dramatic profile of the bridge's steelwork; Roosevelt Island provides another perspective on the structure.
Why it matters
Opened in 1909, the bridge uses a cantilever system rather than the suspended main spans of the Brooklyn, Manhattan and Williamsburg bridges.

05 / 06Hudson panorama

George Washington Bridge

Washington Heights ↔ Fort Lee · 1931 · Suspension

Here the city opens into the Hudson Highlands' dramatic gateway: a broad river, steep Palisades, and a suspension bridge that seems to span an entire landscape.

Walk it
As of October 15, 2026, pedestrians use the rebuilt South Walk; cyclists normally use the North Walk. The Port Authority lists overnight cleaning closures and occasional shared-path arrangements.
Photograph it
The renovated South Walk includes new viewing areas. On the New Jersey side, Fort Lee Historic Park adds a landscape perspective of the Hudson and the bridge.
Why it matters
The Port Authority reopened its rebuilt pedestrian South Walk in October 2026 with accessible approaches and dedicated viewing spaces.
Find Fort Lee Historic Park, New Jersey on a map ↗Official source ↗
06 / 06The oldest chapter

The High Bridge

Washington Heights ↔ Bronx · 1848 · Aqueduct bridge

Before New York built its iconic suspension bridges, it brought clean water across the Harlem River. The graceful arches of the High Bridge tell the quieter story of a growing city.

Walk it
NYC Parks lists daily hours of 7 a.m.–10 p.m., with access via Highbridge Park and the Bronx; signed accessible ramps are available from both boroughs.
Photograph it
From Highbridge Park, look for the rhythm of the old aqueduct arches. This is an excellent stop for a study of nineteenth-century infrastructure.
Why it matters
Built in 1848 as part of the Old Croton Aqueduct, the bridge once delivered water into Manhattan; it now welcomes walkers and cyclists.
