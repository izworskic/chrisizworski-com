Follow the river

Four chapters in a much larger story

01 / COMMERCE

A staircase for a working river

When you see a towboat and barges, you are watching a link in a much longer journey. Grain, fuel and other cargo travel a staircase of locks between the lower Columbia and the interior. Follow a vessel on the map, then remember that its next move belongs to its crew and the lockmaster—not to the dot on your screen.

02 / ENGINEERING

Two water levels, one enormous chamber

The John Day lock sits beside a hydroelectric powerhouse and spillway. The dam controls a broad upstream reservoir—Lake Umatilla—while the downstream Columbia remains much lower. The 113-foot project figure describes maximum lock lift, not a constant daily difference; the federal lock-characteristics inventory separately lists a reference lift of 110 feet. Its 675-foot chamber has 650 feet of published usable length.

03 / ECOLOGY

Salmon moving against the current

The barges are not the only travelers here. Returning adult salmon and steelhead move upstream through fish ladders, while young fish face a downstream journey through a river changed by dams. Follow the fish-passage reports as another chapter in this place, alongside fisheries science and the perspectives of Columbia River Tribes.

04 / HISTORY

A changed landscape

The concrete tells only part of the story. John Day was dedicated in 1968 and completed in 1971, within a Columbia landscape shaped by Indigenous fisheries, communities and generations of change. Keep the projects distinct as you explore: Celilo Falls was submerged by the reservoir behind The Dalles Dam, not John Day.

Why the published lock dimensions differ

Federal Lock Characteristics inventory differentiates overall chamber dimensions (675 × 86 ft) from usable dimensions (650 × 84 ft). Maximum project lift is published as 113 ft; the inventory's reference lift field lists 110 ft. None establishes present water level or navigational clearance. Federal lock characteristics ↗.
