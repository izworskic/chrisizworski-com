How to read the morning

A strong flight is only the first clue.

The useful decision comes from combining independent evidence: meaningful overnight movement, active recent reports at nearby hotspots, notable sightings and weather that lets you bird effectively.

Strong migration + active hotspots

This is the highest-interest combination. Start with nearby hotspots showing recent species activity rather than assuming every park under the radar path received birds.

Strong migration + rough weather

The flight can still matter, but wind and rain may reduce field comfort or visibility. Weather can also concentrate migrants locally, which this tool does not claim to predict.

Lower migration + good local reports

Still worth considering. Resident birds, lingering migrants, notable sightings and smaller movements can make a good morning even without a BirdCast “High” label.

Source boundaries

No invented birding percentage.

  • BirdCast: Cornell Lab radar-based nocturnal migration estimates supply regional movement evidence such as migration amount, direction, speed and altitude when available.
  • eBird species records: Recent public API records add species context. They reflect birder effort and are not a census of everything present.
  • Hotspot ranking: Nearby eBird hotspots are ordered by how many distinct recently reported species are represented in the current data, then report recency, then distance. We do not infer abundance from checklist volume.
  • Notable birds: eBird's notable feed provides unusual recent reports separately from general hotspot activity.
  • Weather: National Weather Service hourly forecasts provide local morning rain and wind context. Our “good / mixed / poor” field-weather label is a transparent rule, not a BirdCast or eBird rating.
  • Season: BirdCast's live feed operates March 1–June 15 and August 1–November 15. Outside those windows, live radar is reported as off-season instead of being fabricated.
  • Species: Weather radar cannot identify exact species in flight. Species names shown here come from recent ground observations, not from the radar signal.

Sources: BirdCast, eBird, National Weather Service, and U.S. Census TIGERweb geography.
