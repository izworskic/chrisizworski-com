A QUICK FIELD GUIDE

Three things to check before launch day

01

When is it actually launching?

Look for a real launch window, not just a date on a calendar. Even a precise time can shift; check the operator again as launch day approaches.

02

Where can you legally watch?

A beach may close, a parking lot may fill, or a visitor center may need special tickets. Find a public spot and check its rules before you drive.

03

Will you see it or only hear it?

Clouds, fog and the direction the rocket flies matter. A few miles can change your view, and the loudest moment may arrive after the rocket is already climbing.

Your rocket-launch questions, answered

What does NET mean on a launch schedule?

NET means no earlier than. It is the earliest published target, not a promise the rocket will fly then. A launch window may last minutes or hours, and teams can stop or restart a countdown. Look for the newest operator update as the time approaches.

Will I hear the engines from the viewing area?

Often, yes, especially when you are near enough and the weather cooperates. You may see the flame before the engine roar reaches you because light travels far faster than sound. A returning booster can sometimes bring a separate sonic boom later, but not every rocket or landing does.

What should I bring to watch a launch?

Bring patience first: rockets can wait on the pad through long holds. Water, layers, sun protection and a chair or blanket can make the wait easier where the venue allows them. Check parking, restroom access, pet rules and bag restrictions. Have a second public viewing place in mind if your first choice fills or closes.

Does this list every U.S. launch?

It covers scheduled missions recorded by Launch Library 2 at the selected coastal launch ranges. Some defense tests, Wallops sounding rockets, Hawaiʻi range activities and launches announced at short notice are not published in that feed. The official range and NASA schedules linked for each region remain essential.

Can I get close to the launch pad?

Do not assume so. Vandenberg, Kodiak and Barking Sands have controlled range access. Starbase closes Boca Chica Beach and Highway 4 around certain tests and launches. Kennedy Space Center viewing is venue and ticket specific. Always follow official admission and safety rules.

What does the 45th Weather Squadron's PoV mean?

It measures the forecast chance of violating one of the covered launch-weather rules during the published window. A 30% PoV does not mean a rocket has a 70% chance of launching. Rocket hardware, range safety, upper winds and recovery conditions can introduce other constraints. We display this number only when an official PDF can be matched to your exact Florida mission.

If it is clear here, will I see the rocket?

No. A forecast for your location does not model the rocket’s trajectory through multiple cloud layers. It also does not predict upper-level launch-weather constraints. The tool intentionally leaves conditions unknown when source evidence is insufficient.

What happens when a launch is scrubbed?

A scrub means the launch attempt has been stopped. It may be weather, a technical issue, or a range restriction, and the next attempt might be hours or days later. Don't assume your parking spot, ticket or launch time carries over. Watch the operator's announcement for the next window before making another trip.
