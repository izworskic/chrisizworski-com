How the flying odds are built

The odds come from every scheduled domestic flight the Bureau of Transportation Statistics recorded in the six days before and after Thanksgiving and Christmas Day in 2018, 2019 and 2022–2025: about 3 million flights on 2,995 routes. 2020 and 2021 are left out because airlines flew radically reduced schedules. Nothing is a crowd count or a survey.

  • Your deadline, not a delay rate: the question is whether you arrive before the time you set. A two-hour delay is harmless with a day to spare and fatal with an hour to spare, so the same flight gets very different odds depending on your plans.
  • Route and time: for each holiday-relative day and departure window, the page uses how often flights were cancelled, diverted, or arrived later than your buffer. Each season counts equally, so one terrible year cannot hide. Your airport and route then adjust that baseline, pulled toward average when a route has few flights. In tests that held out one season at a time, the route-level estimate ranked routes better than an airport-only estimate in every season.
  • If it’s cancelled: for every flight on your route, the data shows when later flights on the same airline actually left. Holiday flights are full, so the odds assume you get on the second one, not the first, and that everyone cancelled on that route in the previous 24 hours is ahead of you in line (one more flight for each). On a meltdown day like December 2022 that queue is long, and each season is scored with its own rebooking conditions.
  • Ranges, not decimals: results are shown in bands (under 1%, 1–3%, 3–5%, 5–10% and so on) because the model is not precise enough to justify more digits.
  • Connections: the first leg must land in time to make your layover with 35 minutes to spare. If it doesn’t, you need a later flight from the hub. A single missed connection is assumed to get the next flight with a seat (you are one passenger, not a full cancelled plane), stretched in bad seasons like any rebooking.
  • First flight of the day: tail numbers show whether each departure was the aircraft’s first trip that day.
  • Live check: in the seven days before your flight, the page asks the National Weather Service for forecasts and active warnings at each airport on your trip. It does not change the historical odds. It tells you when the worst-season figure is the one to plan around.
  • Source data: BTS Reporting Carrier On-Time Performance (flight-level records) and OurAirports for airport locations. The build script and method notes are in the project repository (scripts/holiday-travel/, docs/HOLIDAY_TRAVEL_DATA.md).

What it does not know

  • Your airline and fare. Odds pool every airline on a route, and a meltdown at one (Southwest in December 2022) can dominate a season. Rebooking onto other airlines, which some fares and loyalty tiers allow, is not modeled. Neither are fares or seat availability for the suggested fixes.
  • Two-stop trips and routes with few holiday flights. Only nonstop and one-stop trips on routes with enough holiday flights are scored.
  • This year’s weather, staffing or schedule. Past patterns are not a forecast, and the live check reports NWS forecasts without converting them into odds. Christmas falls on a different weekday each year; the page does not adjust for the weekday layout.
  • Security lines. TSA does not publish usable hourly or per-airport data, so no checkpoint wait is estimated. Arrive per your airline’s guidance.
  • Driving. The driving view still uses AAA/INRIX 2025 timing guidance and EIA fuel prices (AAA Thanksgiving 2025, AAA year-end 2025, EIA weekly gasoline, Oct 12, 2026). There is no free route-level traffic history, so driving days are broad estimates, not odds. National checkpoint volumes from TSA are not used.

The travel calendar rolls over automatically; the cited data remains dated until verified newer official figures are added.

Important: these are historical odds, not a promise. Weather, local congestion and cancellations can override every pattern shown here. If it is a long drive, do not depart so early that you are fatigued.
