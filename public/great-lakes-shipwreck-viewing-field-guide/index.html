Maritime Historian & Underwater Preserve Ranger Field Guide · By Chris Izworski

Ghost ships, 40-foot water clarity & how to view them without scuba gear.

Most travelers assume exploring Great Lakes shipwrecks requires drysuits, trimix tanks, and technical scuba certifications. But along Michigan's 3,288 miles of coastline, dozens of 19th-century wooden schooners, steam barges, and ocean freighters rest in 5 to 25 feet of crystal-clear water. Thanks to aggressive mussel filtration and cold freshwater preservation, you can peer directly into open cargo holds, count white-oak hull ribs, and inspect steam boilers from sea kayaks, paddleboards, narrated glass-bottom tour boats, or while walking sandy beach dunes. Below are 12 audited shallow shipwreck viewing sites across Michigan's official underwater preserves, complete with vessel histories, GPS coordinates, paddling launches, and surface wave-clarity rules.

Maritime Historian Field Briefing · Limnology & Preservation

The Clear Water Phenomenon: Why Great Lakes Wrecks Survive in 10 Feet of Water

In ocean saltwater, shallow wooden wrecks are rapidly consumed by shipworms (Teredo navalis) and pulverized by tidal surges. The Great Lakes present an entirely different physical environment: cold, sterile freshwater devoid of wood-boring worms, coupled with an ecological transformation that turned murky harbors into transparent observation windows.

Limnology · Filter Feeders

The Dreissena Clarity Shift

Since the introduction of zebra and quagga mussels in the late 1980s, the upper Great Lakes have undergone extreme oligotrophication. Millions of bivalves filter the water column constantly, stripping suspended algae and creating 30 to 50 feet of horizontal visibility. Places like Thunder Bay, Munising Bay, and the Manitou Passage now boast water clarity rivaling the Caribbean.

Chemistry · Freshwater Freezing

Cold-Water Timber Mummification

Below 40°F, bacterial decay of dense virgin white oak slows to a crawl. In freshwater without marine borers, hand-adzed timber frames, pine keelsons, wooden mast steps, and hand-forged iron spikes remain structurally intact after 150 years under water. You are looking at wooden craftsmanship from the Abraham Lincoln era preserved in natural cold storage.

Optics · Polarization

The Brewster's Angle Secret

Water surfaces reflect sky glare at Brewster's angle (~53° from vertical). When you look down from a kayak cockpit or boat rail, that surface reflection overwhelms your retinas. High-quality polarized sunglasses filter out polarized surface scatter, allowing you to see past the mirror into the dark, detailed silhouettes of submerged hulls.

Coastal Hydrology Field Rule · Surface Physics

The Wave Viewing Window: Calm Surface vs. Blown-Out Water

Surface chop is the primary obstacle to shallow shipwreck viewing. When waves build, capillary ripples act as thousands of tiny curved lenses that refract sunlight in chaos, blinding overhead observers. Check live wave buoys before setting out.

0.0 to 1.0 ft Waves · Glass Mirror

Prime Viewing Window

Glassy, flat lake surface. Zero surface refraction. You can see individual deck bolts, boiler rivets, and cargo gravel in 20 feet of water from your kayak seat. Ideal for photography and snorkeling.

1.1 to 2.0 ft Waves · Moderate Chop

Polarized Lenses Essential

Gentle wave action creates surface scatter. Hull outlines and large boilers remain visible through polarized sunglasses, but fine structural details blur. Kayaking requires open-water paddling comfort.

2.1+ ft Waves · Blown Out

Overhead Viewing Blocked

Whitecaps and breaking surf suspend near-shore sand and silt, reducing visibility to zero. Kayak exploration is hazardous. Pivot to shoreline wash wrecks on foot (like the Joseph S. Fay at Forty Mile Point).

Alger Underwater Preserve · Lake Superior

The Bermuda (1860 Wooden Schooner)

Depth: 10–25 ft (Deck at 12 ft) Glass-Bottom Boat · Kayak · Snorkel Murray Bay, Grand Island

Built in 1860 in Oswego, New York, the 135-foot two-masted canal schooner Bermuda was hauling 488 tons of Marquette iron ore when she sprang a leak and foundered in October 1870. Salvagers towed her into the sheltered waters of Murray Bay at Grand Island, where she slipped beneath the surface and settled upright on a brilliant white sand shelf.

Today, the Bermuda is arguably the most intact shallow-water wooden schooner in North America. Her cabins, deck beams, cargo hatches, and iron ore cargo remain in place, bathed in transparent, ice-cold Lake Superior water that keeps the wood as sound as the day she went down.

THE MOVE

Book the 2-hour commercial Shipwreck Tours glass-bottom cruise out of Munising for narrated, floodlit overhead viewing. Paddlers can launch a sea kayak from Sand Point Beach on calm mornings (<1 ft waves) and paddle 2.5 miles along Grand Island's southern shore into Murray Bay. Tie off to the yellow Alger Preserve mooring buoy—never drop an anchor into the wooden deck.

Alger Underwater Preserve · Lake Superior

The Herman H. Hettler (1910 Wooden Steamer)

Depth: 10–25 ft Glass-Bottom Boat · Kayak · Snorkel Grand Island East Channel

On November 23, 1926, the 210-foot wooden bulk freighter Herman H. Hettler was seeking shelter in Munising Harbor during a blinding 50 mph snowstorm carrying 1,100 tons of bulk table salt. She missed the channel and slammed onto Trout Point reef. While storm waves tore the hull apart and the salt dissolved into the lake, her massive machinery was left behind.

Her massive Scotch marine boiler, heavy steam cylinders, steering quadrant, and white-oak floor ribs lie resting on a golden sandstone bottom directly in front of the historic 1868 Grand Island East Channel wooden lighthouse.

THE MOVE

Launch a sea kayak from the public ramp at Powell Point or Sand Point Beach in Munising. Paddle north into the East Channel on early summer mornings when winds blow from the south. Hover your kayak directly over the boiler; through polarized sunglasses, you can count the fire tubes and follow the massive propeller shaft stretching into the sand.

Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore · Lake Superior

Au Sable Point Shipwreck Trail (Sitka & Gale Staples)

Depth: 0–10 ft (Beach Wash & Breakers) From Shore & Dunes · Snorkel Hurricane River to Au Sable Light

You do not need a boat to touch 19th-century Great Lakes shipwrecks. Along Lake Superior's dreaded "Shipwreck Coast," the 272-foot wooden steamer Sitka (lost 1904) and 277-foot steamer Gale Staples (lost 1918) broke apart on the shallow sandstone reefs of Au Sable Point. Today, lake waves constantly churn the shoreline sand, exposing hundreds of feet of massive oak bilge timbers, iron drift bolts, and copper fasteners.

Sections of the wooden hull lie right on the dry beach swash line, while larger sections rest in knee-deep to waist-deep water just 30 feet offshore, creating a surreal walking museum where Lake Superior surf breaks through historic frames.

THE MOVE

Park at the Hurricane River Campground day-use lot. Take the 1.5-mile trail east along the beach toward Au Sable Light Station. Look for the black iron spikes and heavy timber frames poking out of the sand dunes and surf. Wear wading shoes or water sandals to walk right up to the 100-foot hull section resting in 2 feet of transparent water.

Manitou Passage Underwater Preserve · Lake Michigan

The Francisco Morazan (1922 Ocean Freighter)

Depth: 0–15 ft (Stands 15 ft Above Water) From Shore & Dunes · Kayak · Snorkel South Manitou Island, Sleeping Bear

On November 29, 1960, the 246-foot Liberian ocean freighter Francisco Morazan was steaming from Chicago to the Atlantic with 940 tons of general cargo when a blinding 50-knot gale and snowstorm blinded her navigators. She grounded 300 feet off the southern shore of South Manitou Island, landing directly on top of the submerged wooden hull of the 1903 steamer Walter L. Frost.

Her steel bow, bridge deck, and cargo masts stand high above the turquoise waters of Lake Michigan. Today she serves as one of the most photographed ghost ships on the continent, with double-crested cormorants and herring gulls nesting in the rusted iron superstructure.

THE MOVE

Take the passenger ferry from Leland to South Manitou Island. Hike the 2-mile South Beach trail through cedar woods to the dune bluff directly overlooking the ship. On calm summer mornings with south/southwest winds under 8 knots, paddle a sit-on-top kayak or snorkel the outer hull perimeter. Do not board the vessel—the rusted steel is razor-sharp and structurally compromised.

Manitou Passage Underwater Preserve · Lake Michigan

The Three Brothers (1888 Wooden Steam Barge)

Depth: 5–45 ft (Bow at 5–7 ft) From Shore & Dunes · Kayak · Snorkel Sandy Point, South Manitou Island

Built in 1888 in Milwaukee, the 162-foot wooden steam barge Three Brothers was carrying 530,000 board feet of hardwood when she sprang a dangerous leak in a September 1911 gale. Her captain intentionally ran her ashore at Sandy Point on South Manitou Island. She was buried under shifting beach sands for 85 years until fierce winter storms in 1996 dramatically uncovered her hull.

Her massive wooden bow stem rests in just 5 to 7 feet of transparent turquoise water only 50 feet from the dry beach sand, sloping down to her stern at 45 feet. The site offers world-class snorkeling directly off the beach.

THE MOVE

Pack a mask, snorkel, and fins on the morning ferry from Leland to South Manitou. Walk 10 minutes east from the ferry dock along Sandy Point beach. Look out into the clear water for the dark silhouette of the hull just 15 yards out. Wade in and snorkel directly over the intact oak stem and heavy decking.

40 Mile Point Lighthouse / Presque Isle · Lake Huron

The Joseph S. Fay (1871 Wooden Steam Barge)

Depth: 0–12 ft (Hull on Dry Beach Sand!) From Shore & Dunes · Kayak · Snorkel Forty Mile Point, Rogers City

The 216-foot wooden bulk freighter Joseph S. Fay was hauling iron ore down Lake Huron on October 19, 1905, when an autumn gale sheared off her rudder. The ship grounded hard just north of Forty Mile Point Lighthouse. Today, an intact 134-foot section of her starboard wooden hull wall rests high and dry directly on the sand beach 200 yards north of the lighthouse tower.

Visitors can literally walk up to and touch the 150-year-old white-oak framing, heavy ceiling planks, and hand-forged iron drift spikes on foot. Her submerged lower hull, keelson, and iron ore cargo lie in 10 to 18 feet of water just 100 feet offshore.

THE MOVE

Drive to Forty Mile Point Lighthouse County Park north of Rogers City (free admission). Take the wooden boardwalk down to the shoreline and walk north 200 yards along the gravel. You will walk right up to the massive wooden hull section on the sand. On calm mornings, wade out with a mask and snorkel to inspect the submerged engine bed and lower ribs.

Thunder Bay National Marine Sanctuary · Lake Huron

The Portland (1863 Wooden Schooner)

Depth: 6–10 ft From Shore & Dunes · Kayak · Snorkel Besser Natural Area, Bell Bay

Built in 1863 in Cleveland, the 150-foot two-masted schooner Portland was driven ashore during an October 1877 northeast gale while attempting to shelter in False Presque Isle Bay. Her white-oak bottom framing, keelson, centerboard trunk, and bilge planks rest over bright white silica sand in just 6 to 10 feet of gin-clear Lake Huron water.

The protected curve of Bell Bay shelters the site from heavy southern swells, making it one of the premier stand-up paddleboarding and snorkeling shipwrecks in the Thunder Bay National Marine Sanctuary.

THE MOVE

Park at the Besser Natural Area trailhead north of Alpena. Walk the 0.75-mile historic ghost town trail through towering white pines to Bell Bay's sandy beach. Launch a paddleboard or swim 100 yards straight out to the yellow seasonal NOAA mooring buoy; look down to trace the entire 150-foot outline through 8 feet of transparent water.

Thunder Bay National Marine Sanctuary · Lake Huron

The Monohansett (1872 Wooden Steam Barge)

Depth: 18 ft Glass-Bottom Boat · Kayak · Snorkel Misery Island / Thunder Bay

The 160-foot steam barge Monohansett was hauling 500 tons of coal on November 23, 1907, when a fire broke out in her boiler room. Her crew steered for Misery Island in Thunder Bay, where she burned to the waterline and sank. Her massive firebox boiler, intact four-bladed cast-iron propeller, steering quadrant, rudder, and charred lower frames remain resting in 18 feet of clear water.

The Monohansett is the marquee stop on commercial glass-bottom boat tours leaving downtown Alpena, allowing passengers to look directly down into her mechanical heart without getting wet.

THE MOVE

Board the Lady Huron glass-bottom catamaran at the Great Lakes Maritime Heritage Center in Alpena for a narrated 2-hour cruise. Experienced sea kayakers can launch from Partridge Point on calm days (<1 ft waves) and paddle 2.5 miles out to NOAA Buoy #7 to snorkel over the towering steam boiler.

Thunder Bay National Marine Sanctuary · Lake Huron

The Shamrock (1875 Wooden Steam Barge)

Depth: 11 ft Kayak & SUP · Snorkel Thomson Beach, Alpena

On June 27, 1905, the 147-foot wooden steam barge Shamrock was entering Alpena with a load of coal when fire erupted in her cargo hold. Her crew grounded her in shallow water off Thomson Beach to abandon ship. Today, the Shamrock lies in just 11 feet of water less than 200 yards off Alpena's city beach.

Because of its shallow depth and proximity to shore, the Shamrock is one of the easiest and most beginner-friendly open-water shipwreck kayak trips in Michigan. On calm sunny days, you can see her entire 147-foot outline from your kayak cockpit.

THE MOVE

Put in directly from the sandy beach at Alpena's Thomson Beach park (Blair Street Pier). Paddle 180 yards east toward the seasonal NOAA mooring buoy. Put on polarized sunglasses to cut the surface glare; follow the wooden keel line and examine the iron rudder hardware resting on the sandy floor.

Straits of Mackinac Underwater Preserve · Lake Huron

The L.B. Coates (1874 Wooden Schooner)

Depth: 8–10 ft Kayak & SUP · Snorkel Moran Bay / St. Ignace Shallows

The 125-foot three-masted wooden schooner L.B. Coates spent nearly a half-century carrying lumber across the Great Lakes before being retired and abandoned in 1921. Her heavy centerboard box, keelson timbers, and bilge ribs rest parallel to the St. Ignace shoreline in protected Moran Bay in only 8 to 10 feet of clear water.

Protected from open Straits swells by the St. Ignace harbor arms, this site allows paddlers and snorkelers to glide directly over 19th-century schooner construction in calm, shallow water.

THE MOVE

Launch from Kiwanis Beach or the St. Ignace public boat launch early in the morning before commercial ferry wakes build in the bay. Paddle east along the shoreline; look straight down through 8 feet of water to trace the centerboard box and heavy oak ribs embedded in the sand.

Straits / Drummond Island Underwater Network · Lake Huron

The Troy & Silver Spray (1872 Schooner & 1894 Tug)

Depth: 5–12 ft (Ribs Protrude Above Water!) Kayak & SUP · Snorkel Whitney Bay, Drummond Island

In Whitney Bay on the southern shore of Drummond Island, an outer barrier of dolomite islands blocks Lake Huron's ferocious chop, creating glass-smooth water over two historic wrecks: the 173-foot wooden schooner-barge Troy (built 1872) and the 54-foot steam tug Silver Spray (built 1894).

The Troy sits in only 8 feet of water, and at normal lake levels her upper wooden ribs literally break the water surface. Just 150 yards away in 10 feet of water, the Silver Spray lies with her steam engine and boiler clearly visible from a kayak cockpit.

THE MOVE

Take the Drummond Island vehicle ferry from DeTour Village. Drive to the Whitney Bay public launch or Yacht Haven. Launch a kayak for an easy 1-mile paddle across glassy bay waters. Glide right alongside the protruding wooden ribs of the Troy and peer down at the tug engine machinery.

Whitefish Point Underwater Preserve · Lake Superior

Whitefish Point & Vermilion Shallows (Comet & Saturn)

Depth: 0–25 ft (Beach Wash to 25 ft) From Shore & Dunes · Kayak · Snorkel Whitefish Point & Vermilion Beach

Known as the "Graveyard of the Great Lakes," Whitefish Point was the mandatory funnel for all vessels entering or leaving Lake Superior. While famous deep casualties like the Edmund Fitzgerald (530 ft) lie 17 miles northwest, ferocious nor'easters frequently wash 19th-century ship timbers, knees, and bronze drift pins out of the sand dunes around the point and Crisp Point.

In the protected lee of Whitefish Bay, shallow vessels like the 147-foot schooner Saturn (25 ft) and 181-foot schooner Comet (20–30 ft) sit in cold, ultra-pure water with iron ore cargo and deck timbers clearly visible on calm summer days.

THE MOVE

Visit the Great Lakes Shipwreck Museum and historic 1861 lighthouse at Whitefish Point. Walk the gravel point where storms uncover heavy timber wash-ups. On calm summer mornings when Lake Superior wave buoys report wave heights under 1.0 foot, launch a sea kayak into the sheltered lee of Whitefish Bay to explore near-shore preserves.

Ranger Golden Rules: Michigan Bottomlands Law & Paddler Safety

1. Michigan Part 761 Law: It Is a Felony to Take Artifacts

Under Michigan Natural Resources and Environmental Protection Act (Part 761), all shipwrecks and submerged cultural materials belong to the State of Michigan. Removing a bolt, spike, timber, or relic is a state felony. Look, photograph, paddle over, but leave everything in place for future generations.

2. Cold Water Shock: Lifejackets & Wetsuits Mandatory

Lake Superior and northern Lake Huron surface temperatures rarely exceed 55°F to 60°F, even in mid-July. Capsizing in 45°F water can cause cold-water shock and gasping reflexes within 60 seconds. Wear an inherently buoyant PFD at all times; use a 3mm to 5mm wetsuit when snorkeling.

3. Never Anchor Directly into Shipwreck Timbers

Dragging a steel anchor through 150-year-old white-oak frames tears apart historic hulls. Always tie off to the official yellow preserve mooring buoys maintained by NOAA and the Michigan Underwater Preserve Council, or drift over the site with a buddy keeping watch.

4. The 1-Foot Wave Rule for Kayaks

Shallow shipwreck viewing requires looking down while keeping your kayak balanced. If wave buoys report chop over 1.0 to 1.5 feet, surface reflection blurs the wreck and side-waves increase capsize risk. If waves build, head back to shore immediately.

Frequently Asked Questions: Great Lakes Shipwreck Viewing

Can you see Great Lakes shipwrecks without scuba diving gear?

Yes. While deep wrecks require technical scuba diving, dozens of 19th- and 20th-century wooden schooners, steam barges, and steel freighters lie in 5 to 25 feet of water. Thanks to Dreissena mussel water filtration, horizontal visibility in northern Lake Michigan, Lake Huron, and Lake Superior frequently reaches 30 to 50 feet. On calm summer mornings, you can view intact hull timbers, machinery, and cargo directly from kayaks, paddleboards, commercial glass-bottom tour boats, snorkeling, or by walking along coastal beach dunes.

Why is water over Great Lakes shipwrecks so extraordinarily clear?

Two primary factors drive this crystal clarity: the cold, oligotrophic (low-nutrient) nature of the upper Great Lakes, and the introduction of invasive Dreissena filter-feeding mussels (zebra and quagga mussels) since the late 1980s. A single adult mussel can filter over one liter of water per day, removing suspended phytoplankton and particulate matter. In protected embayments like Munising Bay, Grand Traverse Bay, and Thunder Bay, this creates an aquarium-like optical window down to 30+ feet.

Is it legal to collect wood, spikes, or artifacts from Michigan shipwrecks?

No. Under Part 761 (Aboriginal Records and Antiquities and Submerged Cultural Resources) of Michigan's Natural Resources and Environmental Protection Act (NREPA), all shipwrecks and submerged cultural artifacts resting on state bottomlands belong to the State of Michigan. Removing, altering, or possessing any shipwreck timber, bronze fastener, or iron relic is a state felony punishable by imprisonment and severe fines. In National Lakeshores like Sleeping Bear and Pictured Rocks, federal 36 CFR regulations strictly forbid disturbing or taking any natural or historical specimen. Look, photograph, but never touch or take.

What wave conditions do I need to view shipwrecks from a kayak or paddleboard?

You need open-lake wave heights under 1.0 foot for optimal glass-mirror viewing. When wave height exceeds 1.5 feet, surface chop shatters the optical refraction window, scattering daylight and creating surface glare that obscures bottom details. Always consult NOAA wave buoys before launching; if waves exceed 2 feet, save open-water paddling for another day and visit beach-wash wrecks like the Joseph S. Fay or Au Sable Point instead.
