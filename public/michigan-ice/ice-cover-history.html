Great Lakes ice cover today, against 54 years of record

Great Lakes ice cover today means the percentage of the five Great Lakes' combined surface that NOAA's Great Lakes Environmental Research Laboratory estimates is ice-covered. The daily NOAA GLERL analysis tracks broad lake and basin coverage, not thickness, strength, or conditions at a shoreline. Across 54 recorded ice years (1973 to 2026) in the daily NOAA climatology, the basin's average coverage reaches its seasonal high around February 19, at 41.3 percent. That is an average of measured ice cover on a calendar date, not a forecast for this winter or a promise that any lake will freeze. A single day's percentage can swing dramatically from year to year because cold air, water temperature, wind, storms, and ice movement interact differently across shallow bays and deep open water. The record also has days without an observation. Those gaps are not zeros. Use the dated live comparison below for the current year, and check local official conditions separately before making any decision near ice.

No ice is safe ice. Everything on this site is accumulated weather data and satellite observation. None of it measures the ice under your feet. The Michigan DNR does not recognize a reliable inch thickness rule and states that ice on the bays of the Great Lakes will always be more fragile. Test with a spud bar or auger every few steps, every trip, and read the ice safety page before you go out.

Why the comparison changes from lake to lake

Shallow Lake Erie responds quickly to a sustained freeze, while deep water in Superior and Michigan can remain open much longer. Wind can spread, break or compress floating ice. Ontario, Huron and their sheltered bays have different shapes and exposure. A basin average does not describe an individual harbor or bay.

Ice years are labeled by their January year. A 2026 ice year includes November and December 2025. The 54-year file includes years 1973 through 2026, but early and late dates have fewer measured values. Those gaps remain missing, not zero. The rank counts only winters measured on the same date.
