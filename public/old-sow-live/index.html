What is the Old Sow Whirlpool?

Old Sow is a huge tidal whirlpool in Western Passage, the narrow channel between Deer Island, New Brunswick, and Eastport, Maine. NOAA describes it as the largest whirlpool in the Western Hemisphere.

It forms when the incoming Bay of Fundy tide pushes an enormous volume of water through this confined passage. The current makes a sharp turn, crosses a deep trench, flows past an underwater mountain, and meets countercurrents. Part of that moving water can then pinch off into a giant rotating current — Old Sow — with smaller whirlpools, often called “piglets,” forming around it.

It is not a permanent hole.The shape and location change with each tidal cycle. Sometimes it looks like a broad spinning patch of rough water rather than a deep funnel.
It can be enormous.NOAA says Old Sow has been measured at more than 250 feet across — roughly the length of a soccer field.
The tide creates it.That is why timing your visit matters: the incoming tidal current has to build before the water becomes most energetic.

The name is usually linked to the grunting or sucking sound of the churning water — either like a sow feeding, or from the old word “sough.” NOAA: What is Old Sow? ↗

What time is Old Sow best?

Public visitor guidance has a useful simple rule: plan around the incoming tide, roughly two to three hours before high tide. The exact appearance varies from cycle to cycle because Old Sow is moving tidal turbulence, not a fixed hole in the water.

This planner goes one step farther. It compares the nearby NOAA flood-current prediction with Eastport high tide, daylight and forecast visibility, then chooses a practical land-viewing window. That is why the recommended time may be more useful than simply looking up high tide and subtracting two hours.

What will you actually see?

The realistic target is energetic, visibly confused water: broad rotation, boils, seams, troughs and smaller eddies turning around the main turbulent area. Those features can be impressive even when there is no clean funnel.

A dramatic deep vortex is possible, but it is not the normal shape every cycle. NOAA describes Old Sow as variable in size and form; the visible whirlpool shifts as the tidal current changes.

This is not a boating route planner

NOAA warns that Old Sow’s turbulent water can be dangerous to small craft. This page helps people plan a land-viewing visit. It does not recommend a launch time, route through Western Passage, or a “safe” transit window.

Why did the tool choose this time?

Step 1: deterministic NOAA math. Nearby NOAA current predictions create the only eligible flood-current windows. Tide, current speed, daylight and weather timestamps are never invented by a model.

Step 2: JEV recommendation. The private JEV harness may rank only those fixed windows for a visitor using near-term timing, daylight and forecast visibility. Old Sow Whirlpool questions

What is the best time to see Old Sow?

Use the live recommendation at the top of this page. As a general visitor rule, the productive period is around the incoming tide, commonly described as roughly two to three hours before high tide.

Where is the best land view?

Deer Island Point in New Brunswick. The turbulent area shifts offshore, so the map marks the viewpoint rather than inventing a permanent whirlpool pin.

How long should I stay?

Allow roughly 60 to 90 minutes around the recommended window. The water builds and changes; an exact peak-current timestamp is not an appointment time for a single visible funnel.

Is Old Sow always visible?

No. Tide and current make some periods much more promising than others, but the visible form changes. Eddies and boils are more dependable targets than a textbook funnel.

Is this live?

The NOAA tide/current and NWS weather inputs are current forecast/observation products. The page does not claim to have a live camera or sensor directly observing Old Sow itself.
